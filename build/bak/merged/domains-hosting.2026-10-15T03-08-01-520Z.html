A / AAAA for production web300–3600Balances CDN cacheability against failover time

4.2 The effective minimum TTL

Value Model

The TTL that actually applies is the lowest value any hop in the chain will accept.

Four separate limits combine to produce the effective TTL: the record’s published value, the zone apex SOA minimum field on servers that treat it as a default, the cache-size limit of intermediate resolvers, and per-record overrides enforced by the authoritative provider.

# What TTL does a public resolver actually honour?
$ dig @8.8.8.8 www.example.com A +noall +answer
www.example.com.   284   IN  A   198.51.100.20

$ # Authoritative answer shows the published TTL
$ dig @ns1.dns-provider.net www.example.com A +noall +answer
www.example.com.   3600  IN  A   198.51.100.20

# The 284 above is a cached TTL counting down. Both are correct.

Measure, do not assume. Run the dig command above before and after a change. If the recursive answer does not differ from the authoritative answer, an intermediate cache is pinning the record beyond your intent, and the change will not land on schedule.

Forcing specific resolvers to re-read a record.

# Bypass every local cache and query authority directly
$ dig @ns1.dns-provider.net example.com SOA +norecurse

# Inspect a specific public resolver's cache
$ dig @1.1.1.1 www.example.com A +noall +answer

# Validate the zone served by every nameserver
$ for ns in ns1.dns-provider.net ns2.dns-provider.net; do
  echo "== $ns"; dig @$ns example.com SOA +short
done

# Flushing a CDN edge requires provider API access, not DNS
# Most CDNs expose a per-zone purge API; DNS TTL reduction
# does not evict a cached HTML document already fetched.

DNS TTL is not a cache-busting mechanism for content. It controls how long a browser or CDN re-resolves. It has no effect on an object already cached at an edge node. Content purging and DNS propagation are separate operations and need separate tools.

4.5 TTL discipline before a migration

The correct sequencing when a hostname must change provider.

StepActionWait
1Set target A/AAAA and CNAME records to the current values at 300 s TTL24 h
2Confirm the new target serves the content correctly over HTTPS—
3Validate the new certificate, SPF, DKIM and DMARC on the new target—
4Cut over A/AAAA or the CNAME at the apex≤ 300 s
5Verify from at least three external resolvers—
6Hold at 300 s for 72 h, then restore the normal TTL72 h

Pre-stage everything. Every record that will be needed at cutover should exist at the destination provider at 300 s TTL before the switch. Cutover should then be a single record change, not a sequence of them.

IPv4 and IPv6 address records, and the ordering that matters for dual-stack sites.

; Happy Eyeballs requires multiple A/AAAA on the SAME name
www.example.com.     300 IN  A     198.51.100.20
www.example.com.     300 IN  A     203.0.113.20
www.example.com.     300 IN  AAAA  2001:db8:20::20

Never publish an AAAA record unless IPv6 is genuinely functional end to end. An unreachable AAAA record causes happy-eyeballs connections to stall for the IPv6 attempt timeout before falling back to IPv4, which is the single most common self-inflicted IPv6 performance defect.

A records must round-robin manually. Unlike a load balancer, DNS performs no health checking and no session affinity. Two A records are a round-robin only, not failover. A record whose backend is down will still receive one in N client requests.

Aliases, and the rule that makes them invalid at the zone apex.

A CNAME maps one name to another canonical name, and the resolver follows the target and discards any records at the aliasing name. Because DNS cannot hold a CNAME alongside other rdata, a CNAME is forbidden at the apex of any zone. A provider-specific ALIAS or ANAME record is a server-side synthesised answer that achieves the same result legally.

; Subdomain CNAME is fine
shop.example.com.      300 IN  CNAME  shops.example.net.
www.example.com.       300 IN  ALIAS  www.vendor-cdn-edge.net.

; DNAME redirects an entire subtree, plus its descendants
legacy.example.com.    300 IN  DNAME  archive.other-domain.net.

CNAME chasing is depth-limited. RFC 1034 §4.3.2 step 3a stops the resolver after encountering a CNAME loop; most implementations also cap total chain length. Chasing a CNAME from www.example.com to a CDN hostname to an origin hostname is three lookups before your TTL is even considered.

Mail exchange records and the service-location records layered on top.

; Lower preference number wins; 0 is the MX "null" exchanger
@                      IN  MX  10 mx1.mail-provider.net.
@                      IN  MX  20 mx2.mail-provider.net.
@                      IN  MX  0   .

; SRV: priority weight port target
_sip._tcp.example.com. IN  SRV 10 20 5060 sip1.example.net.
_sip._tcp.example.com. IN  SRV 20 20 5060 sip2.example.net.
_submission._tcp.example.com. IN SRV 10 10 587 mail.example.net.
_imap._tcp.example.com.      IN SRV 10 10 143 imap.example.net.

MX targets must be hostnames, never IP addresses, and never a CNAME. An MX pointing at a CNAME is silently non-conforming and many receiving MTAs will refuse it.

Reuse the SPF-approved hosts. Your MX destinations must be exactly the hosts listed in your SPF include:, or mail from those hosts will fail SPF. Configure the two in the same change.

3.5 TXT, CAA, NS and SOA

Records that carry policy rather than addresses.

; Multiple TXT records on one name are valid and MUST be concatenated
; by DNS-aware systems into one logical string.
_sip._tcp.example.com. IN TXT "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail-provider.net -all"

; CAA constrains which CAs may issue for this domain
example.com.           3600 IN CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.           3600 IN CAA 0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.           3600 IN CAA 0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"

; The mandatory apex record pair
example.com.           3600 IN NS   ns1.dns-provider.net.
example.com.           3600 IN NS   ns2.dns-provider.net.
example.com.           3600 IN SOA  ns1.dns-provider.net. hostmaster.example.com. (
                                 2026092901 ; serial - increment on every change
                                 7200       ; refresh   - secondary retry interval
                                 3600       ; retry     - failed refresh retry
                                 1209600    ; expire    - secondary stops answering
                                 3600 )     ; negative  - SOA cache TTL for NXDOMAIN

CAA is inherited from the parent. A CAA record at example.com. applies to every subdomain that does not declare its own. Publishing CAA once at the apex is usually correct; publishing it on a subdomain that only lists one CA blocks every other CA for that name.

SOA serial discipline. Use RFC 1912 date-based serials: YYYYMMDDnn. Incrementing the serial is what tells secondaries that the zone changed. Forgetting it is the most common cause of secondary servers serving stale data indefinitely.

The three-party model that separates policy ownership from the customer contract.

A registry owns the TLD’s parent zone and the rules for registering names beneath it. A registrar is accredited by that registry and holds the customer-facing contract. A reseller purchases service from a registrar at wholesale prices and sells it onward, adding support and branding layers.

LayerHoldsControls
RegistryThe parent zone, registration data, escrowTLD eligibility rules, price ceilings, dispute policy
RegistrarRegistrar–customer contract, authInfo, accountNameservers, DNSSEC DS, transfer state, contact data
ResellerSubcontract with an upstream registrarMarkup, support tier, branding; no protocol control

WHOIS is not authoritative. Registrars may suppress, thin or proxy WHOIS output under ICANN § 4.10 and the 2024 European GDPR addendum. RDAP (RFC 9082, 9083, 9084) is the machine-readable replacement and is the only interface that reliably exposes transfer state and object events.

The anatomy of a fully qualified domain name.

A domain name is a sequence of labels separated by dots, read right to left. The rightmost label is the TLD, the label immediately to its left is the registered domain, and everything to the left of that is controlled by the registrant.

# api.staging.eu.example.com.
# |   |        |   |       |   |
# |   |        |   |       |   +-- root zone, always empty label
# |   |        |   |       +------ TLD: com
# |   |        |   +-------------- registered domain: example
# |   |        +------------------ second level: eu (registry run)
# |   +----------------------- third level: staging (registrant controlled)
# +--------------------------- host: api

A fully qualified domain name (FQDN) ends with a trailing dot, which explicitly denotes the root zone. Configuration files almost always require the trailing dot to be present; omitting it causes the resolver to append the local search or domain suffix from /etc/resolv.conf.

Trailing dot discipline. In every zone file and server block, write zone names and delegated targets as absolute names with a trailing dot: example.com. and ns1.example.com. Otherwise BIND and nginx treat the value as relative and append the server’s own origin, producing names such as ns1.example.com.example.com.

The character and length constraints every hostname must satisfy.

Hostnames must consist only of letters, digits and hyphens (LDH). RFC 1123 permits a leading digit; earlier RFC 952 did not. Underscores are tolerated in DNS but are not legal in hostnames, and their presence breaks TLS certificate hostname validation and most web server routing.

Case on the wire. RFC 4343 makes DNS case-insensitive, but the 0x20 encoding that randomises letter case is used for DNS transaction ID entropy. Do not rely on preserving case in comparisons; compare with a case-folded comparison.

How a resolver walks from the root to your nameserver.

Resolution begins at the DNS root, a fixed set of anycast addresses published in the root hints file and distributed inside the resolver itself. The root does not answer A queries for your zone; it answers with a referral naming the TLD’s authoritative servers.

# Trace the full delegation path
$ dig +trace www.example.com A

;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: NOERROR, id: 38421
;; flags: qr rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 0, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1

;; QUESTION SECTION:
;www.example.com.        IN  A

;; AUTHORITY SECTION from root:
;.                       IN  NS  a.root-servers.net.
;.                       IN  NS  b.root-servers.net.

;; AUTHORITY SECTION from com. -> referral to gTLD servers:
;com.                    IN  NS  a.gtld-servers.net.
;com.                    IN  NS  b.gtld-servers.net.

;; AUTHORITY SECTION from gTLD -> referral to your nameservers:
;example.com.            IN  NS  ns1.example.com.
;example.com.            IN  NS  ns2.example.com.
;; ADDITIONAL (glue):
;ns1.example.com.        IN  A   198.51.100.10
;ns2.example.com.        IN  A   198.51.100.10

Each step in that chain is a separate network round trip. This is the structural reason resolution is slow on first contact and fast afterwards: only the answer is cached, not the path.

In-bailiwick in-addr.arpa is not special. Delegation works identically for reverse zones. A failure to delegate 10.51.100.198.in-addr.arpa to your nameserver breaks reverse lookups but has no effect on forward resolution of www.example.com.

From a browser to a connection, a name is resolved through a fixed sequence. Understanding the sequence explains why a change takes time to appear and why different tools disagree during that window.

# Watch the whole resolution, and the cache that short-circuits it.
dig +trace example.com

# What the recursive resolver returns, with flags and TTL.
dig example.com A
;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: NOERROR, id: 41234
;; flags: qr rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 1, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1
;; ANSWER SECTION:
;example.com.       3600    IN    A   203.0.113.10
;; Query time: 24 msec

# Ask a specific resolver, bypassing the local cache entirely.
dig @1.1.1.1 example.com A +noall +answer
dig @8.8.8.8 example.com A +noall +answer
StepWho is askedCacheableControlled by
1The stub resolver in the OSYes, per /etc/hosts and any local cacheThe machine
2The recursive resolverYes, for the record TTLThe resolver operator
3The root serversYes, by TLDThe registry
4The TLD nameserversYes, by delegationThe registry
5The domain’s authoritative nameserversAnswer TTLThe DNS host
6Connect to the returned addressNot DNSThe hosting provider

The practical consequence is that a DNS change is never instant. The old answer remains valid for up to the previous TTL, at every caching layer independently, and caches frequently ignore a lowered TTL until the record has been seen with the new value once. This is why a migration lowers the TTL at least a day in advance, and why two resolvers can legitimately return different answers for several hours after a correct change.

A stale cache is not a bug and there is no way to flush it from the client side. The only reliable lever you control is the TTL on the record you are about to change. If a name must move urgently, do not attempt to shorten the TTL at the moment of the outage — shorten it in advance, wait for the old value to expire everywhere, and only then make the change.

1 · The Domain Name System

How domain names are structured, constrained and delegated, from the DNS root down to an authoritative nameserver.

1.1 Labels, the root and FQDN

The anatomy of a fully qualified domain name.

A domain name is a sequence of labels separated by dots, read right to left. The rightmost label is the TLD, the label immediately to its left is the registered domain, and everything to the left of that is controlled by the registrant.

# api.staging.eu.example.com.
# |   |        |   |       |   |
# |   |        |   |       |   +-- root zone, always empty label
# |   |        |   |       +------ TLD: com
# |   |        |   +-------------- registered domain: example
# |   |        +------------------ second level: eu (registry run)
# |   +----------------------- third level: staging (registrant controlled)
# +--------------------------- host: api

A fully qualified domain name (FQDN) ends with a trailing dot, which explicitly denotes the root zone. Configuration files almost always require the trailing dot to be present; omitting it causes the resolver to append the local search or domain suffix from /etc/resolv.conf.

Trailing dot discipline. In every zone file and server block, write zone names and delegated targets as absolute names with a trailing dot: example.com. and ns1.example.com. Otherwise BIND and nginx treat the value as relative and append the server’s own origin, producing names such as ns1.example.com.example.com.

1.2 LDH rule and length limits

The character and length constraints every hostname must satisfy.

Hostnames must consist only of letters, digits and hyphens (LDH). RFC 1123 permits a leading digit; earlier RFC 952 did not. Underscores are tolerated in DNS but are not legal in hostnames, and their presence breaks TLS certificate hostname validation and most web server routing.

1.4 The delegation chain

How a resolver walks from the root to your nameserver.

Resolution begins at the DNS root, a fixed set of anycast addresses published in the root hints file and distributed inside the resolver itself. The root does not answer A queries for your zone; it answers with a referral naming the TLD’s authoritative servers.

# Trace the full delegation path
$ dig +trace www.example.com A

;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: NOERROR, id: 38421
;; flags: qr rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 0, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1

;; QUESTION SECTION:
;www.example.com.        IN  A

;; AUTHORITY SECTION from root:
;.                       IN  NS  a.root-servers.net.
;.                       IN  NS  b.root-servers.net.

;; AUTHORITY SECTION from com. -> referral to gTLD servers:
;com.                    IN  NS  a.gtld-servers.net.
;com.                    IN  NS  b.gtld-servers.net.

;; AUTHORITY SECTION from gTLD -> referral to your nameservers:
;example.com.            IN  NS  ns1.example.com.
;example.com.            IN  NS  ns2.example.com.
;; ADDITIONAL (glue):
;ns1.example.com.        IN  A   198.51.100.10
;ns2.example.com.        IN  A   198.51.100.10

Each step in that chain is a separate network round trip. This is the structural reason resolution is slow on first contact and fast afterwards: only the answer is cached, not the path.

In-bailiwick in-addr.arpa is not special. Delegation works identically for reverse zones. A failure to delegate 10.51.100.198.in-addr.arpa to your nameserver breaks reverse lookups but has no effect on forward resolution of www.example.com.

3 · Resource Records

3.1 Record anatomy

The five fields of a master zone record.

;  owner              TTL   class  type  rdata
www                     3600  IN     A     198.51.100.20

The owner name may be written in full, or given as a bare label if it shares the origin domain. The class IN (Internet) is effectively the only class in public use; CH and HS exist but no modern provider serves them. The TTL is discussed in chapter 4.

3.2 A and AAAA

IPv4 and IPv6 address records, and the ordering that matters for dual-stack sites.

; Happy Eyeballs requires multiple A/AAAA on the SAME name
www.example.com.     300 IN  A     198.51.100.20
www.example.com.     300 IN  A     203.0.113.20
www.example.com.     300 IN  AAAA  2001:db8:20::20

Never publish an AAAA record unless IPv6 is genuinely functional end to end. An unreachable AAAA record causes happy-eyeballs connections to stall for the IPv6 attempt timeout before falling back to IPv4, which is the single most common self-inflicted IPv6 performance defect.

A records must round-robin manually. Unlike a load balancer, DNS performs no health checking and no session affinity. Two A records are a round-robin only, not failover. A record whose backend is down will still receive one in N client requests.

3.3 CNAME and DNAME

Aliases, and the rule that makes them invalid at the zone apex.

A CNAME maps one name to another canonical name, and the resolver follows the target and discards any records at the aliasing name. Because DNS cannot hold a CNAME alongside other rdata, a CNAME is forbidden at the apex of any zone. A provider-specific ALIAS or ANAME record is a server-side synthesised answer that achieves the same result legally.

; Subdomain CNAME is fine
shop.example.com.      300 IN  CNAME  shops.example.net.
www.example.com.       300 IN  ALIAS  www.vendor-cdn-edge.net.

; DNAME redirects an entire subtree, plus its descendants
legacy.example.com.    300 IN  DNAME  archive.other-domain.net.

CNAME chasing is depth-limited. RFC 1034 §4.3.2 step 3a stops the resolver after encountering a CNAME loop; most implementations also cap total chain length. Chasing a CNAME from www.example.com to a CDN hostname to an origin hostname is three lookups before your TTL is even considered.

3.4 MX, SRV and autodiscover

Mail exchange records and the service-location records layered on top.

; Lower preference number wins; 0 is the MX "null" exchanger
@                      IN  MX  10 mx1.mail-provider.net.
@                      IN  MX  20 mx2.mail-provider.net.
@                      IN  MX  0   .

; SRV: priority weight port target
_sip._tcp.example.com. IN  SRV 10 20 5060 sip1.example.net.
_sip._tcp.example.com. IN  SRV 20 20 5060 sip2.example.net.
_submission._tcp.example.com. IN SRV 10 10 587 mail.example.net.
_imap._tcp.example.com.      IN SRV 10 10 143 imap.example.net.

MX targets must be hostnames, never IP addresses, and never a CNAME. An MX pointing at a CNAME is silently non-conforming and many receiving MTAs will refuse it.

Reuse the SPF-approved hosts. Your MX destinations must be exactly the hosts listed in your SPF include:, or mail from those hosts will fail SPF. Configure the two in the same change.

3.5 TXT, CAA, NS and SOA

Records that carry policy rather than addresses.

; Multiple TXT records on one name are valid and MUST be concatenated
; by DNS-aware systems into one logical string.
_sip._tcp.example.com. IN TXT "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail-provider.net -all"

; CAA constrains which CAs may issue for this domain
example.com.           3600 IN CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.           3600 IN CAA 0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.           3600 IN CAA 0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"

; The mandatory apex record pair
example.com.           3600 IN NS   ns1.dns-provider.net.
example.com.           3600 IN NS   ns2.dns-provider.net.
example.com.           3600 IN SOA  ns1.dns-provider.net. hostmaster.example.com. (
                                 2026092901 ; serial - increment on every change
                                 7200       ; refresh   - secondary retry interval
                                 3600       ; retry     - failed refresh retry
                                 1209600    ; expire    - secondary stops answering
                                 3600 )     ; negative  - SOA cache TTL for NXDOMAIN

CAA is inherited from the parent. A CAA record at example.com. applies to every subdomain that does not declare its own. Publishing CAA once at the apex is usually correct; publishing it on a subdomain that only lists one CA blocks every other CA for that name.

SOA serial discipline. Use RFC 1912 date-based serials: YYYYMMDDnn. Incrementing the serial is what tells secondaries that the zone changed. Forgetting it is the most common cause of secondary servers serving stale data indefinitely.

TTL 0 is not honoured. RFC 2181 §5.2 states that a TTL of zero may be treated as the minimum value the receiving system permits. Several public resolvers and almost all SMTP receivers enforce a floor of 300 seconds or more. Setting a low TTL is not the same as setting none.

4.2 The effective minimum TTL

The TTL that actually applies is the lowest value any hop in the chain will accept.

Four separate limits combine to produce the effective TTL: the record’s published value, the zone apex SOA minimum field on servers that treat it as a default, the cache-size limit of intermediate resolvers, and per-record overrides enforced by the authoritative provider.

# What TTL does a public resolver actually honour?
$ dig @8.8.8.8 www.example.com A +noall +answer
www.example.com.   284   IN  A   198.51.100.20

$ # Authoritative answer shows the published TTL
$ dig @ns1.dns-provider.net www.example.com A +noall +answer
www.example.com.   3600  IN  A   198.51.100.20

# The 284 above is a cached TTL counting down. Both are correct.

Measure, do not assume. Run the dig command above before and after a change. If the recursive answer does not differ from the authoritative answer, an intermediate cache is pinning the record beyond your intent, and the change will not land on schedule.

4.4 Cache inspection and flushing

Forcing specific resolvers to re-read a record.

# Bypass every local cache and query authority directly
$ dig @ns1.dns-provider.net example.com SOA +norecurse

# Inspect a specific public resolver's cache
$ dig @1.1.1.1 www.example.com A +noall +answer

# Validate the zone served by every nameserver
$ for ns in ns1.dns-provider.net ns2.dns-provider.net; do
  echo "== $ns"; dig @$ns example.com SOA +short
done

# Flushing a CDN edge requires provider API access, not DNS
# Most CDNs expose a per-zone purge API; DNS TTL reduction
# does not evict a cached HTML document already fetched.

DNS TTL is not a cache-busting mechanism for content. It controls how long a browser or CDN re-resolves. It has no effect on an object already cached at an edge node. Content purging and DNS propagation are separate operations and need separate tools.

The correct sequencing when a hostname must change provider.

5 · DNSSEC

Cryptographic authentication of DNS answers, the trust chain that anchors it, and the operational procedure to deploy it safely.

5.1 What DNSSEC signs

DNSSEC authenticates origin and integrity. It does not encrypt or provide availability.

DNSSEC appends signatures to a zone’s records, letting a validating resolver prove that an answer came from the domain’s authoritative server and was not modified in transit. It does not hide the answer, does not prevent spoofing at the application layer, and cannot stop an attacker who controls the authoritative server.

PropertyDNSSEC provides
Origin authenticationYes — the answer originates from the signed zone
IntegrityYes — modification causes SERVFAIL, not a forged answer
ConfidentialityNo — answers remain in cleartext
AvailabilityNo — validation failure is fail-closed for the validating resolver

5.2 The chain of trust

How a resolver anchors trust from the root down to your zone.

The root zone is signed with a Key Signing Key (KSK). Each TLD publishes a Delegation Signer (DS) record in the parent zone pointing to that TLD’s KSK, and each TLD signs its own zone. Your registrar publishes a DS record for your zone in the parent zone, which is the single act that extends the chain of trust to your domain.

# The DS record at the parent is the only piece you control externally
example.com.   IN  DS   370 13 2 BE98F9D0F3C1C2E4A7B6D5C4F3E2D1C0B9A88776655443322110099887766
                            |  |  | |
                            |  |  +-+-- digest type 2 = SHA-256
                            |  +---- algorithm 13 = ECDSAP256SHA256
                            +------- key tag of the zone's KSK

Do not publish a DS without a working signature. A DS record for an unsigned zone causes every validating resolver to return SERVFAIL for the entire domain. This is a full outage, not a degradation. Sign the zone and verify it independently before submitting the DS.

5.3 DNSSEC record types

RRSIG, DNSKEY, NSEC/NSEC3 and the relevant flags.

; DNSKEY: the public keys present in the zone
example.com.  IN  DNSKEY 257 3 13 "kR+oKm3Ln0G6q1sZ2lE..."   ; KSK, SEP flag 257
example.com.  IN  DNSKEY 256 3 13 "T7YpQ0x9Lm2vB8cN..."   ; ZSK, no SEP flag

; RRSIG: one per record set, covering type, algorithm, labels, TTL,
; original TTL, expiration, inception, key tag, signer name, signature
www.example.com. 3600 IN RRSIG A 13 3 3600 20261001000000 20260901000000 12345 example.com. "abc..."
;                 ^^ ^^ ^                                             ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
;              type alg labels   origTTL  expiration     inception          key tag

; NSEC3: hashed denial of existence, adds unguessable opt-out behaviour
example.com.  IN  NSEC3PARAM 1 0 12 a1b2c3d4

RRSIG expiration is the hard failure deadline. A signature that has passed its expiration makes the resolver return SERVFAIL, which is why key rollovers and clock skew are genuine operational risks.

5.4 Enabling DNSSEC

The safe ordering for a managed-DNS deployment.

StepActionRisk if out of order
1Confirm the DNS provider supports automatic signingManual key management is error-prone
2Enable signing in the DNS provider; obtain key tags and digests—
3Validate the zone from an independent validating resolver—
4Publish the DS record at the registrarPublishing DS before step 2 causes SERVFAIL
5Re-verify from multiple public validating resolvers—
# Unmanaged BIND: sign the zone, then publish the DS at the registrar
$ dnssec-keygen -a ECDSAP256SHA256 -f KSK -n ZONE example.com
$ dnssec-keygen -a ECDSAP256SHA256 -f ZSK -n ZONE example.com
$ named-checkzone example.com /etc/bind/zones/db.example.com
# Zone must be reloaded after keys are published in the zone itself.

Monitor expiration dates. Alert on RRSIG inception and expiration timestamps. A rollover that silently fails presents as a domain-wide SERVFAIL with no other diagnostic, usually discovered by users.

5.5 Verifying a signed zone

The commands that prove validation is working end to end.

# ad flag set = the resolver validated the answer cryptographically
$ dig +dnssec example.com A
;; flags: qr rd ra ad; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 2, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1
example.com.   3600 IN  A    198.51.100.20
example.com.   3600 IN  RRSIG A 13 3 3600 ...

# Validate from several public validating resolvers independently
$ for r in 8.8.8.8 1.1.1.1 9.9.9.9 208.67.222.222; do
  printf '%-16s ' $r
  dig @$r +dnssec +short example.com A | head -1
done

# Confirm the chain of trust with delv
$ delv example.com @8.8.8.8 +rtrace

Negative results matter. If dig +dnssec returns the answer but no RRSIG, the zone is unsigned. If delv reports no DS at the parent while a zone DNSKEY exists, the domain is insecure for that resolver even though signing is enabled in the DNS provider console.

Rollback path. If DNSSEC is causing SERVFAIL, remove the DS record at the registrar first. Removing it returns the domain to the insecure state immediately at most resolvers. Deleting the zone signing keys while DS remains is the worst possible ordering.

6.3 Secondary DNS

A real second authoritative server, configured in the zone itself.

Adding a second NS record that points at another server of the same provider is not failover. True secondary DNS requires a second server configured as a secondary of the zone, transferring it over AXFR or IXFR with a shared TSIG key.

# Primary zone: declare a secondary and a notify address
example.com.  IN SOA ns1.example.com. hostmaster.example.com. (
              2026092901 7200 3600 1209600 3600 )
example.com.  IN NS  ns1.example.com.
example.com.  IN NS  ns2.secondary-dns.net.
example.com.  IN MX 10 ns1.example.com.

ns2.secondary-dns.net.   IN A 203.0.113.53
example.com.             IN A 203.0.113.53   ; glue for the in-zone NS

# named.conf on the primary: notify + allow-transfer with TSIG
zone "example.com" {
  type master;
  file "/etc/bind/zones/db.example.com";
  allow-transfer { key "dnssec-secondary"; };
  also-notify { 203.0.113.53; };
};

Transfer security is mandatory. An unrestricted allow-transfer exposes your entire zone to enumeration and, with a poorly configured secondary, to poisoning. Use TSIG, restrict source addresses, and prefer DNSSEC-signed zones over transfer restriction for authentication.

6.4 Anycast and provider diversity

What anycast does and does not protect against.

Anycast announces the same nameserver addresses from many locations, so resolution follows BGP and clients reach a nearby server. This reduces latency and, because anycast works at the network layer, tolerates loss of a single location. It does not protect against provider-wide failure, a BGP incident, or a control-plane outage at the provider.

Correlated failure. Two nameservers at the same provider share a control plane, a network transit provider, and a DNS software stack. A provider bug affecting both names simultaneously is more likely than the 1-in-N independence the record count suggests. Cross-provider nameservers are the only real diversification.

6.5 Splitting DNS across providers

Sub-delegating portions of the namespace to a second provider.

Delegating a subdomain gives that subtree a completely independent NS set and thus an independent failure domain. It is the correct architecture when a service, a team, or a vendor needs an isolated DNS configuration.

; In the parent zone - delegate the subtree
api.example.com.      IN NS  ns1.vendor-dns.net.
api.example.com.      IN NS  ns2.vendor-dns.net.
ns1.vendor-dns.net.   IN A   198.51.100.80   ; glue, required here
ns2.vendor-dns.net.   IN A   198.51.100.81

; In the child zone - fully independent records
; origin example.com, managed entirely by the second provider
api.example.com.      IN A     198.51.100.80
_8443._tcp.api.example.com. IN SRV 10 10 8443 api.example.com.

Good use cases. Sub-delegation suits a vendor-managed API, a staging environment needing different HSTS posture, a high-traffic service that should be able to switch to a CDN without touching the main zone, and any subtree with different change-control requirements.

Glue is not inherited upward. When you delegate api.example.com, the glue for ns1.vendor-dns.net must live in the example.com zone. It will not be found in a parent zone, and nameservers that live inside the delegated subtree cannot serve as its own parents.

8 · Virtual Hosts and Reverse Proxies

How a single IP address serves many domain names, how proxies forward requests, and the TLS handshake implications of the arrangement.

8.1 Name-based routing

Selecting a virtual host from the requested hostname.

HTTP/1.1 and all later versions carry the Host header in every request, so a single listener can route by hostname. The server matches the header against configured server names, applies the most specific match, and serves the corresponding document root. Because the choice is made after the TCP connection and the TLS handshake, one IP and one port can serve an unbounded number of sites.

Name-based virtual hosting has been the HTTP/1.1 default since RFC 2616 §14.23. Requests without a usable Host header can only be served by a default server, which is why a misconfigured default block is the usual cause of an unrelated domain landing on your site.

8.2 nginx server blocks

A complete, production-shaped nginx configuration for one site.

# Redirect bare domain to the canonical host and enforce HTTPS
server {
    listen 80;
    listen [::]:80;
    server_name example.com;
    location /.well-known/acme-challenge/ { root /var/www/acme; }
    location / { return 301 https://www.example.com$request_uri; }
}

server {
    listen 80;
    listen [::]:80;
    server_name www.example.com;
    location /.well-known/acme-challenge/ { root /var/www/acme; }
    location / { return 301 https://www.example.com$request_uri; }
}

server {
    listen 443 ssl;
    listen [::]:443 ssl;
    http2 on;
    server_name www.example.com;

    ssl_certificate     /etc/letsencrypt/live/www.example.com/fullchain.pem;
    ssl_certificate_key /etc/letsencrypt/live/www.example.com/privkey.pem;
    ssl_protocols       TLSv1.2 TLSv1.3;
    ssl_prefer_server_ciphers off;
    ssl_session_cache   shared:SSL:10m;
    ssl_session_timeout 1d;
    ssl_stapling on;
    ssl_stapling_verify on;

    add_header Strict-Transport-Security "max-age=63072000; includeSubDomains; preload" always;

    root /var/www/example/public;
    index index.php index.html;

    location / {
        try_files $uri $uri/ /index.php?$query_string;
    }

    location ~ \.php$ {
        include fastcgi_params;
        fastcgi_pass unix:/run/php/php8.3-fpm.sock;
        fastcgi_param SCRIPT_FILENAME $document_root$fastcgi_script_name;
        fastcgi_hide_header X-Powered-By;
    }
}

Test before reload. nginx -t validates syntax and catches duplicate server_name conflicts. nginx -T dumps the fully expanded configuration including all includes, which is what you need when the active config is not the file you edited.

8.4 Reverse proxy and headers

Forwarding to an upstream while preserving the client’s identity.

upstream app_pool {
    least_conn;
    server 10.0.1.10:3000 max_fails=3 fail_timeout=30s;
    server 10.0.1.11:3000 max_fails=3 fail_timeout=30s;
    keepalive 32;
}

server {
    listen 443 ssl;
    server_name www.example.com;
    location / {
        proxy_pass http://app_pool;
        proxy_http_version 1.1;

        proxy_set_header Host              $host;
        proxy_set_header X-Real-IP         $remote_addr;
        proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-For   $proxy_add_x_forwarded_for;
        proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-Proto $scheme;
        proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-Host  $host;
        proxy_set_header Connection        "";

        proxy_connect_timeout 5s;
        proxy_send_timeout    30s;
        proxy_read_timeout    30s;
    }
}

Never trust the client’s headers verbatim. $proxy_add_x_forwarded_for appends to whatever the client sent. If your application uses the first element of X-Forwarded-For as the client IP for access control or rate limiting, an attacker can spoof it. Either strip the inbound header at the edge or use a trusted-proxy-aware framework setting.

8.5 Default and catch-all servers

Preventing unknown hostnames from being served your content.

The first server block for a given listen address becomes the default. Any request whose Host matches no block lands there. An explicit catch-all with a default_server flag and a 444 response is the correct posture.

server {
    listen 80 default_server;
    listen [::]:80 default_server;
    server_name _;
    access_log off;
    return 444;   # close connection without a response
}

server {
    listen 443 ssl default_server;
    listen [::]:443 ssl default_server;
    server_name _;
    ssl_reject_handshake on;   # nginx 1.19.4+ : no certificate needed
}

8.6 SNI and multi-domain certificates

How the certificate is selected during the handshake.

Server Name Indication carries the requested hostname in the TLS ClientHello, letting the server choose which certificate to present before the HTTP request exists. SNI is what makes one IP serve many HTTPS sites. It was optional in early TLS, so very old clients that omit it will receive the default certificate regardless of the hostname requested.

SAN only. Since Chrome 58, the common name field is ignored; only Subject Alternative Names are matched. Every hostname a certificate covers must appear in the SAN extension. A certificate without a SAN, or missing one of your hostnames, fails with ERR_CERT_COMMON_NAME_INVALID.

# Inspect the certificate a host actually presents
$ openssl s_client -connect www.example.com:443 -servername www.example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -dates -ext subjectAltName

9.4 certbot and Caddy

Two dominant automated approaches to issuance and renewal.

# certbot: webroot mode, preferred when port 80 is already served
$ certbot certonly --webroot -w /var/www/acme \
    -d example.com -d www.example.com \
    --email ops@example.com --agree-tos --no-eff-email \
    --cert-name www.example.com

# wildcard requires dns-01 with a provider plugin or manual hook
$ certbot certonly --dns-route53 \
    -d example.com -d '*.example.com' \
    --email ops@example.com --agree-tos

# Caddy: automatic internal issuance and renewal, zero config
www.example.com {
    encode zstd gzip
    reverse_proxy 127.0.0.1:3000 {
        header_up X-Forwarded-For {remote_host}
        header_up X-Forwarded-Proto {scheme}
    }

    header {
        Strict-Transport-Security "max-age=31536000; includeSubDomains"
        X-Content-Type-Options "nosniff"
        Referrer-Policy "strict-origin-when-cross-origin"
    }
}

Caddy obtains certificates automatically. It performs ACME issuance on first start and renews at roughly two-thirds of the certificate lifetime. If Caddy cannot bind port 80 or 443, it falls back to the TLS-ALPN challenge, which most restrictive networks also block.

9.5 Renewal and alerting

Proving renewal works before you need it.

$ certbot renew --dry-run --deploy-hook "systemctl reload nginx"
Saving debug log to /var/log/letsencrypt/letsencrypt.log

# Confirm the timer is active
$ systemctl list-timers | grep certbot
Fri 2026-09-30 04:12:00 UTC  certbot.timer  certbot.service

# Expiry alert independent of the renewal mechanism
$ openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 \
    -servername example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -enddate
notAfter=Dec 11 00:00:00 2026 GMT

The classic renewal failure is a deploy-hook that does not run. Renewal succeeds, the new certificate is written, but the running web server keeps serving the old one from memory. Any certificate change needs a reload. Hooks that only fire on renewal, not on initial issuance, are a frequent cause.

Monitor expiry from outside. Alert on certificate notAfter from an independent vantage point, at 30, 14 and 7 days remaining. Internal monitoring that reads the same file the renewal process writes will not notice a renewal that never ran at all.

9.6 Chain assembly and stapling

Why missing intermediates break clients but not your own tests.

A server must send the leaf certificate plus every intermediate needed to reach a trust anchor. Sending only the leaf works on machines that cached the intermediate previously and fails everywhere else. fullchain.pem is the correct file; cert.pem alone is not.

# Count the certificates a server sends in the chain
$ openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com \
    -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | grep -c 'BEGIN CERTIFICATE'
2   # leaf + intermediate, as expected

# Verify the served chain against the system trust store
$ openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com \
    -verify_return_error </dev/null 2>&1 | grep 'Verify return code'
Verify return code: 0 (ok)

OCSP stapling. The server fetches the revocation status once and presents it with the handshake, saving every client a round trip. It requires the full chain and a working outbound connection to the CA. It is deprecated in favour of the smaller Certificate Transparency-based CRL distribution methods, so treat stapling failures as non-critical.

Test from a clean environment. Use a container with an empty trust store, or an external checker. A server that works from your laptop usually works because your machine already has the intermediate cached from a previous visit.

10.1 HTTP/2

Multiplexing, header compression, and the operational obligations it introduces.

HTTP/2 (RFC 9113) multiplexes concurrent streams over a single connection using binary framing, and compresses headers with HPACK. The result is far lower per-request overhead and no head-of-line blocking at the transport layer.

One connection is not free. Because HTTP/2 opens streams without the connection-limit heuristics that governed HTTP/1.1, a client can generate thousands of concurrent requests against a server whose limits were tuned for HTTP/1.1. Review worker, file-descriptor and rate-limit configuration when enabling HTTP/2.

10.2 HTTP/3 and QUIC

QUIC over UDP, and why it changes firewall assumptions.

HTTP/3 runs over QUIC (RFC 9000), which implements its own reliable transport, congestion control and per-stream loss recovery on top of UDP. Streams are independently ordered, so a lost packet on one stream does not block others — eliminating head-of-line blocking entirely.

UDP 443 must be permitted. Blocked UDP 443 causes silent downgrade to HTTP/2 at best and total connection failure at worst. Verify reachability with quiche-client or a CDN synthetic, not with a browser, because browsers fall back silently and you will never notice.

# Confirm HTTP/3 is actually negotiated
$ curl --http3-only -I https://example.com 2>/dev/null | head -1
HTTP/3 200

$ # Compare across protocols
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w 'h3 %{http_version} %{time_connect} %{time_starttransfer}\n' --http3 https://example.com
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w 'h2 %{http_version} %{time_connect} %{time_starttransfer}\n' --http2 https://example.com

10.4 Edge TLS termination

The full TLS path across a proxied deployment.

In a proxied deployment there are two handshakes: client to edge, and edge to origin. Both are independent, and both need valid certificates. The client-side certificate is usually a single universal edge certificate covering shared hostnames, which is why the certificate shown to users is rarely the one you uploaded.

# Verify origin certificate validity from outside the CDN
$ openssl s_client -connect origin.example.net:443 \
    -servername www.example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -dates

# Confirm the end-to-end protocol, not just the edge
$ curl -sI --resolve www.example.com:443:203.0.113.14 \
    https://www.example.com -w '%{http_version}\n'

Full (strict) mode only. Flexible mode encrypts only client-to-edge. Off mode is plaintext throughout. Strict requires a valid certificate on the origin. Use strict unless there is a technical impossibility, and record the reason if you must deviate.

10.5 Protecting and shaping the origin

Hiding the origin address and correcting client IP attribution.

# Preserve real client addresses end to end
proxy_set_header X-Real-IP         $http_cf_connecting_ip;
proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-For   $proxy_add_x_forwarded_for;
proxy_set_header CF-Connecting-IP  $http_cf_connecting_ip;
real_ip_header CF-Connecting-IP;
real_ip_recursive on;

# Origin hardening
firewall {
    allow 10.0.0.0/8          # internal
    allow 203.0.113.0/24     # CDN egress ranges, keep current
    deny all;                 # everything else refused
}

Findable origin addresses bypass the CDN. If the origin IP appears in historical DNS, certificate transparency logs, subdomains or mail headers, attackers can reach it directly. After cutover, restrict the origin firewall to CDN ranges and confirm with curl --resolve from an external address.

Do not let CDN IPs become mail servers. Shared CDN egress ranges overlap with reputation databases. Sending transactional mail from a CDN address damages deliverability for other tenants. Use a dedicated mail provider with its own IPs and reverse DNS.

11 · Control Panels: cPanel, Plesk, DirectAdmin

11.1 What a panel really is11.2 cPanel and WHM11.3 Plesk11.4 DirectAdmin11.5 Automating panel operations11.6 Panel-specific risks

Shared hosting administration panels, their DNS and TLS tooling, and how to drive them without clicking.

11.1 What a panel really is

A panel is an automation layer over cPanel, Apache and BIND, with its own administrative surface.

What the panel actually drives

A control panel does not run its own web server, mail server or DNS server. It writes configuration files for software that is already installed — Apache or nginx for HTTP, BIND or an authoritative daemon for DNS, Postfix or Dovecot for mail, ProFTPd or Pure-FTPd for file transfer, MySQL or MariaDB for storage — and then reloads each service. The panel is a templating and permissions layer over those daemons, plus a database that records what it changed and when. This is why the same CLI tools you would use on a bare VM remain the ground truth: named-checkconf, apachectl configtest, postconf -n and doveadm read the files the panel wrote, not the panel's opinion of them.

# Where the panel's work actually lands - the daemons it drives
$ ls -la /usr/local/apache/conf/httpd.conf /etc/named.conf /etc/dovecot/dovecot.conf 2>&1 | head
-rw-r--r-- 1 root root 21830 Sep 26 03:14 /usr/local/apache/conf/httpd.conf
-rw-r--r-- 1 root root  4102 Sep 26 03:14 /etc/named.conf
-rw-r--r-- 1 root root 11204 Sep 26 03:14 /etc/dovecot/dovecot.conf

# Validate every zone file the panel has written, then the web and mail stacks
$ named-checkconf -z            # parses all zones; no output means clean
$ apachectl configtest           # 'Syntax OK' means the generated config is coherent
$ postconf -n                    # non-default Postfix settings, including the panel's
$ mysql -e "SHOW DATABASES;"    # one schema per account, all panel-managed

Verifying panel changes from the CLI

Because a panel edit is a file change followed by a service reload, you can prove it happened — and prove it took effect — without opening the UI. Read the file the panel wrote, then query the live service and compare the two. If the file changed but the answer did not, the reload failed silently, which is a more common failure than the edit itself. Treat the panel's confirmation message as a claim, not evidence.

# A panel edit is a file change followed by a reload - prove both halves
$ grep -n 'www' /etc/bind/zones/db.example.com

$ dig @ns1.hosting-provider.net www.example.com A +short
198.51.100.20

$ systemctl status named --no-pager | head -3
   Active: active (running) since Sat 2026-09-26 03:14:12 UTC; 2 weeks ago

The panel as an attack surface

The panel login is the highest-value credential on the account: it can rewrite DNS, read every email address, drop files into every document root and change TLS certificates. Panels therefore carry a long history of authenticated file-write, privilege-escalation and CSRF findings, and the admin URL is scanned continuously. Enforce two-factor authentication, restrict the panel to a management IP range where the provider allows it, and confirm the URL refuses plain HTTP rather than serving a login form over it.

# Probe the panel's URL and TLS posture before trusting it
$ curl -sI https://server.example.net:2083 | grep -iE '^HTTP/|^server|^strict-transport'

# A plain-HTTP panel URL with no redirect is a live credential-leak risk
$ curl -sI http://server.example.net:2083 | grep -iE '^HTTP/|^location'
HTTP/1.1 200 OK

A control panel is a web application that configures the software beneath it: a mail transfer agent, an HTTP server, a database engine, a FTP daemon and usually a DNS server. The panel is not the server. Understanding what each panel actually modifies lets you verify its work with the same CLI tools you would use on a bare VM.

The panel is an attack surface with the highest-value credentials on the account. Panels have a history of authenticated file-write, privilege-escalation and CSRF vulnerabilities. Enable two-factor authentication, restrict access by IP where the provider supports it, and never leave the panel URL reachable over unencrypted HTTP.

12 · Email Infrastructure

12.1 Delivery path12.2 SPF12.3 DKIM12.4 DMARC12.5 Reputation and deliverability

DNS-level mail authentication: how SPF, DKIM and DMARC interact and what each failure actually costs.

12.1 Delivery path

How a message travels from your MTA to the recipient’s.

The sending MTA looks up the recipient domain’s MX records, connects to the receiving MTA, and performs a TLS handshake according to the receiving server’s advertised policies. Post-2020 policy requires TLS at every hop; STARTTLS opportunistic encryption alone no longer earns deliverability credit.

# Check the receiving domain's requirements
$ dig +short MX gmail.com
10 aspmx.l.google.com.
dig +short TXT _dmarc.gmail.com
"v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@google.com"

# Confirm your own MTA presents a valid certificate
$ openssl s_client -starttls smtp -connect mx1.mail-provider.net:25 \
    -servername mx1.mail-provider.net </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -dates

Port 25 is frequently blocked. Many residential ISPs and cloud providers block outbound SMTP on port 25. If submission on 587 succeeds but port 25 is blocked, your mail will be queued indefinitely. Verify port 25 egress explicitly before committing to a provider.

12.3 DKIM

Cryptographic signing of message content and headers.

; One public key per selector; rotate by adding a new selector
mailselector1._domainkey.example.com. 300 IN TXT (
  "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIGfMA0GCSqGSIb3DQEBBQUAA4GNADCBiQKBgQ..."
  ; 2048-bit key; rsa-sha256 and 4096-bit both accepted
)
mailselector2._domainkey.example.com. 300 IN TXT (
  "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIGfMA0GCSqGSIb3DQEBBQUAA4GNADCBiQKBgQC..."
)

; Header on the wire
DKIM-Signature: v=1; a=rsa-sha256; c=relaxed/relaxed; d=example.com;
 s=mailselector1; h=from:to:subject:date:message-id;
 bh="SHA256 body hash"; b="RSA signature over headers+body"

Relaxed alignment for both headers and body is the only practical configuration: forwarding breaks strict body alignment, and strict header alignment breaks when an ESP rewrites the From header.

Rotate DKIM keys without downtime. Publish the new selector, configure it as the active signing key, wait until a full message lifetime has passed (48 hours is the common figure), then remove the old selector. Any single message will carry at least one signature that validates.

12.4 DMARC

Publishing a policy and acting on the reports.

; Roll out p=none then quarantine then reject
_dmarc.example.com. 300 IN TXT (
  "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com; ruf=mailto:dmarc@example.com; fo=1; pct=100"
)
_dmarc.example.com. 300 IN TXT (
  "v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com; fo=1; pct=100"
)
_dmarc.example.com. 300 IN TXT (
  "v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com; adkim=s; aspf=s; fo=1; pct=100"
)
TagPurpose
p=none, quarantine or reject. The only mandatory tag
rua=Where aggregate XML reports are sent
ruf=Where individual forensic reports are sent
fo=Which failures trigger a report
pct=Percentage of messages the policy applies to, for staged rollout
adkim= / aspf=Alignment mode; s is strict, r relaxed
ad=dkim or spf to select a fallback when one fails

Align on the From domain, not the envelope. DMARC passes if either SPF or DKIM passes and is aligned with the visible From domain. Mail from a third-party ESP without an aligned DKIM signature and a matching SPF record will fail regardless of both mechanisms passing individually.

13.2 The transfer procedure

The ordered steps, with the failure point of each.

13.4 Nameserver and DNS continuity

How to guarantee the site stays up during a transfer.

A transfer changes only the registrar. If DNS is hosted by a third party and the nameservers are not modified during the transfer, resolution is completely unaffected. If DNS is hosted at the losing registrar, the nameservers are normally reset to the gaining registrar’s defaults at completion, which will break the site.

$ dig +short NS example.com

ns2.dedicated-dns.net.

$ dig example.com ANY +noall +answer > /tmp/pre-transfer-zone.txt
$ dig AXFR example.com @ns1.dedicated-dns.net > /tmp/pre-transfer-axfr.txt

Prefer out-of-bailiwick nameservers. If nameservers live outside the zone, they survive both a transfer and a DNS provider change without glue to manage. This is the strongest argument for a dedicated DNS provider, as covered in chapter 6.

Run a registrar account audit quarterly. List every login, every registered domain, every API token and every active session. Delete tokens you cannot attribute. This takes fifteen minutes and reliably finds stale access.

15.3 Authority problems

NXDOMAIN, NODATA and empty answers, distinguished precisely.

$ # NXDOMAIN: the name itself does not exist
$ dig typo.example.com A +noall +comments +authority
;; status: NXDOMAIN, id: 2201
example.com.  300 IN SOA ns1.dns-provider.net. hostmaster.example.com. 2026092901 7200 3600 1209600 3600

# NOERROR with an empty ANSWER = NODATA: the name exists in another
# type, but has no record of the type you asked for
$ dig www.example.com MX +noall +comments
;; status: NOERROR, id: 2202
;; AUTHORITY SECTION:
example.com.  300 IN SOA ns1.dns-provider.net. ...

# SERVFAIL: the server could not answer - look at DNSSEC or upstream
$ dig example.com A +noall +comments
;; status: SERVFAIL, id: 2203

Negative caching explains "it worked a minute ago". An NXDOMAIN response is cached for the SOA minimum value, not the record TTL. A typo that was fixed still returns NXDOMAIN until the negative cache expires, commonly 300 to 3600 seconds.

15.4 SERVFAIL and DNSSEC

Tracing a validation failure to its origin.

$ # Which part of the chain fails validation?
$ dig +dnssec example.com A +comments
;; status: SERVFAIL, id: 41923
;; WARNING: RRSIGs missing for example.com

$ # If the zone is signed, is the DS present at the parent?
$ dig +short DS example.com @a.gtld-servers.net
# empty output here with a signed zone = broken chain

$ # Trace validation decisions resolver by resolver
$ delv example.com @8.8.8.8 +rtrace
;; fully validated

$ # Compare a validating against a non-validating resolver
$ dig @8.8.8.8 example.com A +short
$ dig @1.1.1.1 example.com A +short   # Cloudflare validates

SERVFAIL with an unsigned answer is the signature of a broken DS. A DS record published for a zone that is not signing, or an RRSIG past its expiration, causes validating resolvers to fail closed. Remove the DS at the registrar first; it is the fastest and safest recovery.

15.5 HTTPS and certificate problems

Reading the negotiation without a browser in the way.

$ # Full TLS diagnostic in one command
$ openssl s_client -connect www.example.com:443 -servername www.example.com \
    -verify_return_error </dev/null 2>&1 | grep -E 'Verify return code|Protocol|Cipher|verify (error|subject|issuer)'

# Protocol downgrade check

# HTTP response and redirect behaviour
$ curl -sIL --max-redirs 5 http://example.com | grep -iE '^HTTP|^location|^strict-transport'

Test SNI explicitly. Without -servername, openssl s_client receives the default certificate, which for many setups is unrelated to the hostname you typed. A certificate that looks correct in one command and wrong in another is almost always an SNI artefact.

15.6 Mail delivery problems

Testing DNS policy and delivery end to end.

# Does your own SPF policy permit the sending host?
$ dig +short TXT example.com | grep spf1
$ dig +short TXT mail-provider.net | grep spf1   # resolve the include

# Send a test message and read the full authentication result
$ swaks --to ops@partner.example --from noreply@example.com \
    --server smtp.mail-provider.net --h-Subject test --body test \
  | tail -20
# Deliver to a Gmail and open the raw message to read:
# Authentication-Results: ... spf=pass ... dkim=pass ... dmarc=pass

# Delivery path to the recipient domain
$ host --tries 3 recipient-domain.com
recipient-domain.com has MX mx1.recipient-domain.com ...

MX preference is not a load balancer. Recipients try MX records in ascending priority order and fall through only on failure. Two MX records at priority 10 will send all mail to the first one until it breaks. Use priority 10 and 20 with independent hosts for genuine distribution.

Always publish a null MX. For a subdomain with no mail, IN MX 0 . declares that mail is explicitly not accepted. Without it, RFC 5321 §5.1 requires the domain owner to publish an address matching the local part, and many senders instead attempt delivery and defer indefinitely.

Plan the write freeze. A single-transaction dump is consistent at dump time, not at transfer time. Anything written between dump and cutover is lost unless the application is in read-only mode during the window. Size the freeze to your write rate.

17.4 Cutover

The ordered sequence, with the TTL discipline that keeps it short.

T-72 hAction
Lower every record TTL to 60–120 s and wait the full previous TTL
Verify target serves correct content over HTTPS from outside

Confirm mail records are correct if mail is in scope
T-0Action

Rollback is a DNS change. Because rollback is a single record flip, keep the source intact and functional for a full 48 hours. Do not decommission on the day of cutover. A rollback performed on day three is still a rollback.

18.1 Unicode on the wire

Why IDNs are transmitted as ASCII labels.

DNS itself carries octets. An internationalised domain name is stored and transmitted as its Punycode (RFC 3492) ASCII representation and labelled with the xn-- prefix. The user-facing Unicode form exists only in browsers, mail clients and applications.

18.2 Punycode conversion

Converting between representations, and always converting in the same direction.

$ # Unicode to Punycode
$ python3 -c "print('münchen.de'.encode('idna').decode())"
xn--mnchen-3ya.de

$ # Punycode to Unicode (show the raw decoded bytes for clarity)
$ python3 -c "print(b'xn--mnchen-3ya'.decode('idna'))"
münchen

$ # The command-line tool form
$ idn2 münchen.de
xn--mnchen-3ya.de
$ idn2 xn--mnchen-3ya.de
münchen.de

# DNS always speaks ASCII - query the encoded form
$ dig +short A xn--mnchen-3ya.de

Never decode an IDN twice. Applying a decode transformation to an already-decoded string corrupts it silently and produces a hostname that resolves to nothing. Decode exactly once, at the display boundary, and keep the encoded form everywhere else.

18.3 Scripts, homographs and case

The confusable-character problem and the handling rules that reduce it.

IDN registration opens a homograph attack surface: characters from different scripts render identically, so a domain can visually impersonate another. Registries mitigate with script-mixing restrictions on new registrations and by publishing confusable sets; registries and browsers both apply the UTS #46 mapping rules.

MappingExampleEffect
LowercaseCafé → caféCase is not significant for matching

Nameprepcombining marks normalisedCanonical form must match for registration
Deviation charactersß ↔ ssAccepted in registration, mapped in display

Display both forms for IDN customers. Show the Unicode form as the primary presentation and the Punycode form alongside it. Support tickets and phishing reports become far easier to triage when the exact bytes are visible.
