0.1 What Midjourney is

Midjourney is a hosted text-to-image service: you send it a natural-language prompt and it returns a small batch of images. This section frames what the tool does, what it does not do, and why its output has a recognisable house style that sets it apart from other generators.

What "text-to-image" actually produces

Midjourney is a generative image model. You give it words, it returns pixels, and there is no scene graph, no layer stack and no editable source file behind the result. The output is a raster image — a grid of coloured pixels — so anything you want to change later you either regenerate, or edit with the built-in Editor (Chapter 5). Understanding this early prevents the most common beginner frustration: looking for a "project file" that does not exist.

The model does not retrieve photographs. It has no image database it searches, and it cannot cite a source. It learned statistical relationships between text and images during training and paints a new image from noise, guided by your words. That is why two prompts that read differently but mean the same thing can produce wildly different pictures, and why the same prompt run twice produces different pictures unless you pin a seed (Chapter 6).

Why Midjourney looks different

Every generator is judged on four axes: prompt adherence (does it draw what you asked), aesthetic quality (does the result look good), coherence (do hands, text and physics hold together), and control (how precisely can you steer it). Midjourney historically traded a little literal adherence for a strong default aesthetic: even a careless prompt tends to come back with pleasing light, colour and composition. Competitors such as Stable Diffusion optimise for control and local execution; Midjourney optimises for a fast, tasteful result with minimal setup.

That bias matters when you choose a tool. If you need pixel-exact product photography on a transparent background, a diffusion model you can fine-tune locally may be the better instrument. If you need a hundred evocative concept images this afternoon, Midjourney's default taste is an asset, not a limitation. The rest of this guide teaches you to add control on top of that taste without losing it.

Everything in Midjourney is a conversation between a prompt (what you describe) and parameters (how the model should interpret it). Fluency comes from treating the two separately rather than writing one long sentence and hoping.

0.2 Discord versus the web editor

Midjourney began life as a Discord bot and later gained a first-class web application. Both drive the same model and accept the same parameters, but they suit different jobs. This section explains what each surface gives you so you can pick deliberately.

The Discord surface

The original interface is a Discord server: you type /imagine in a bot channel or a DM and the bot replies with a message containing four thumbnails and a row of buttons. Discord is fast to type in, easy to run in parallel with other work, and it keeps every generation in a scrollable history. Slash commands such as /settings, /info, /blend, /describe and /shorten exist only here, and some power-user features (rapid rerolling, watching other people's prompts in public channels) are native to it.

Its weaknesses are the ones you would expect of a chat client used as an image tool: discovery is poor, organising hundreds of jobs is painful, and the model sees your prompt in a public channel unless you generate in a DM or hold a stealth subscription. Discord also throttles your interaction behind a message queue, so a burst of jobs is slower to fire than a GUI batch.

The web app

The web application at midjourney.com is now the primary interface for most people. It offers a prompt bar with an auto-complete for parameters and style codes, a personal archive you can search and organise into folders, an Explore feed, and — most importantly — the Editor, which does inpainting, outpainting, panning and zooming on a finished image. Those editing operations have no equivalent in Discord. Redeeming fast hours, checking your remaining time and managing billing also live here.

The practical division is: reach for Discord when you want to type a rapid series of prompts, run slash-command utilities, or see what the community is making; reach for the web app when you want to refine an image, organise a project, or work on a laptop without a Discord client open. Your jobs and history are shared across both, so you can start in one and finish in the other.

A stealth (private) subscription hides your images from the public feed. On the basic tiers every generation in a public channel is visible to other members — never paste client work, unpublished designs or personal data into a public server.

0.3 Subscription plans and GPU modes

Midjourney is subscription-only and metered by GPU time, not by image count. The plan you hold determines how many fast hours you get, whether you can relax indefinitely, and whether your work is private. Understanding the metering model stops you burning an allowance by accident.

Fast, Relax and Turbo

Every generation consumes GPU minutes. In Fast mode your job jumps the queue and is billed against your monthly fast-hour allowance. In Relax mode — available on higher tiers — generation is unlimited but queued behind paying traffic, so it can take anywhere from a few seconds to several minutes. Turbo mode, offered on the top tiers, runs faster still and bills fast hours at a higher multiplier. You switch between them with /fast, /relax and /turbo in Discord, or from the mode control in the web app.

The counter-intuitive part is that you pay for time, not for results you like. A failed prompt that returns four useless thumbnails costs the same as a perfect one. This is why experienced users spend their fast hours exploring and their relax hours mass-producing: iterate until the prompt is right, then relax-reroll for volume while you do something else.

Tier (indicative)Fast hours / monthRelaxStealth
Basic~3.3NoNo
Standard~15YesNo
Pro~30YesYes
Mega~60YesYes

Plan prices, fast-hour allotments and the revenue threshold for commercial use change over time and are set by Midjourney, not by this guide. Always confirm current numbers on the official pricing page before you budget a project. Treat the table above as a shape, not a quote.

Stealth and commercial eligibility

Two plan differences matter beyond raw speed. First, stealth mode (Pro and Mega) keeps generations out of the public gallery — effectively mandatory for client or unreleased work. Second, Midjourney's terms grant broader commercial rights to paid tiers, and organisations above a revenue threshold are expected to hold a higher plan to use the images commercially. Chapter 8 covers the licence in detail; the practical takeaway here is that the tier you buy is also a legal decision, not just a throughput one.

0.4 How an image is generated

A short, honest mental model of what happens between pressing enter and seeing four thumbnails. You do not need the mathematics to use the tool well, but understanding the pipeline explains latency, seeds, and why small prompt changes sometimes cause large visual jumps.

Latent diffusion in plain terms

The model starts from random noise — literally television static — and removes it in a sequence of steps, nudged at each step toward an image that a separate text encoder says matches your prompt. This is latent diffusion: the work happens in a compressed representation of the image rather than raw pixels, which is what makes generation take seconds rather than hours. The guidance strength, exposed to you as --stylize, controls how hard the model pushes toward the prompt versus how much it trusts its own trained aesthetic.

Because generation is stochastic, the random starting noise matters. A seed is the number that fixes that starting noise and, together with your prompt and parameters, makes a result reproducible. Change the seed and you get a sibling image with the same ideas; keep it and re-run to get the same picture back. Chaos (--chaos) controls how far apart the four images in a single grid are allowed to drift from one another.

The job pipeline

When you submit a prompt the service enqueues a job. The job records your prompt, your parameters, the model version, the seed and a job id. The queue dispatches it to a GPU, the model renders four low-resolution thumbnails, and the result is delivered back to whichever surface you used. Upscaling and editing are separate jobs spawned from the first — which is why an upscale can queue even when your prompt ran instantly, and why your job history is the canonical record of an image rather than any file you downloaded.

# The lifecycle of one generation, conceptually
prompt + parameters
  -> job queued (gets a seed and an id)
  -> 4 low-res thumbnails rendered
  -> you pick one: upscale / vary / reroll (each a NEW job)
  -> optional: Editor inpaint / outpaint (more jobs)
  -> download the final raster (no editable source exists)

Versions are model generations

Midjourney ships numbered model versions — v5, v6, v7 and so on — each a different trained model with its own strengths. A parameter like --v 7 selects one; the highest default version is used when you omit it. Because the models differ, a prompt tuned on one version will not render identically on another, and some parameters are version-specific. The lesson is to pin a version once a project looks right, so a silent default bump does not change your look mid-series. Parameters and their version caveats get full treatment in Chapter 3.

Every prompt is also a small experiment. Change one variable at a time — the subject, then the lighting, then the parameter — and you will learn the model's behaviour far faster than by rewriting everything at once.

1.1 Joining and the /imagine flow

The mechanics of your first generation, from subscribing to reading the reply. This is the loop you will repeat thousands of times, so it is worth getting the muscle memory right from the start.

Subscribing and finding the bot

You subscribe on the Midjourney website, which links your Discord account. Once subscribed you can either join the official Discord server and post in a newcomer or general channel, or message the Midjourney bot directly to keep your work out of the public scroll. The bot only recognises prompts from subscribed accounts, so a missing reply is almost always an inactive subscription or an expired session, not a broken bot.

The /imagine command

In Discord you type /imagine, choose the prompt field, and enter your description. The bot acknowledges with an "Waiting to start" message and then replaces it with four images and a row of buttons. The verb does not appear in the prompt itself — you are filling a field named prompt, so you write only the description. In the web app there is no command at all: you type into the prompt bar and press enter.

# Discord: the command fills a field, it is not part of the text
/imagine prompt: a weathered fisherman mending a net, golden hour, shallow depth of field, 85mm photograph --ar 3:2

# Web app: identical text, no command prefix
a weathered fisherman mending a net, golden hour, shallow depth of field, 85mm photograph --ar 3:2

Reading the reply

The reply contains the four thumbnails, your original prompt text, and the buttons. Below them sits a line of metadata naming the job — a long alphanumeric string that identifies this exact generation. Copy the prompt text if you want to reuse it, and record the job id when a result matters, because the id is how you find the image again after your history grows.

Newer versions also expose a Draft mode or fast preview path that renders quicker, lower-fidelity images at a fraction of the cost, which you then promote to a full render once you like the composition. Treat drafts as thumbnails to think with, not as deliverables.

1.2 The web app workspace

The web application is more than a mirror of Discord. It adds the archive, the search, the folders and the Editor. Learning its layout turns a firehose of images into a manageable library.

The prompt bar and parameter hints

The web prompt bar behaves like a sophisticated input field. As you type a parameter it suggests valid values, and it can surface style codes and personalisation prefixes so you do not have to memorise them. This matters most for style references (--sref) and personalisation, where a wrong code produces a subtly wrong aesthetic rather than an error message.

The archive, search and folders

Everything you generate lands in your personal archive, searchable by prompt text and filterable by rating, aspect ratio and date. You can sort images into folders and favourite the ones worth returning to. Because the service stores the original and its upscales, the archive is the closest thing Midjourney has to a project file — curate it deliberately, or you will be scrolling through ten thousand thumbnails to find the one lighthouse you liked.

Name or tag work the moment you make it. A folder per project, plus a short note on the prompt that worked, beats any later attempt to reconstruct your thinking from an image alone.

The Explore feed

The public Explore feed is a searchable stream of generations from other members, complete with their prompts. It is the single best source of prompts to study: search a term you care about, read the prompts that produced the images you admire, and you will see how experienced users combine subject, light, medium and parameters. Copy the structure, not the subject.

1.3 The 2×2 grid and upscaling

Midjourney almost always returns four images at once. Why four, what the grid hides, and what "upscale" now means are the three things beginners get wrong most often.

Why a grid of four

A single render is a gamble; four renders at once let you see the spread of possibilities around your prompt in one pass. They are siblings: same prompt, same parameters, different starting noise (unless you fixed a seed). If all four are wrong, the prompt is wrong and you should rewrite it rather than reroll. If one is close, you upscale or vary it. Reading the grid as a distribution of outcomes — not as four independent attempts — is the habit that saves fast hours.

What upscaling means now

The U1–U4 buttons select a thumbnail and produce a larger version. In older versions upscaling was a creative act that added a lot of detail; in current versions it is closer to a clean enlargement plus light refinement, and the real creative refinement happens in the Editor. This is a frequent source of confusion when following older tutorials. Upscaling is still a separate job, so it queues independently of the original.

# Grid buttons, decoded
U1 U2 U3 U4   # upscale that quadrant (enlarge it)
V1 V2 V3 V4   # make variations of that quadrant
Upscale (Subtle / Creative)   # older versions: two flavours of upscale
reroll        # regenerate the whole 4-up with the same prompt

How large the output is

Base thumbnails are small; the upscale yields a larger raster whose exact dimensions depend on the model version and your aspect ratio. For print or large-format work you will still need an external upscaler, because Midjourney's enlarge is about composition and detail, not about reaching 300 dpi at poster size. Download the upscale, keep the prompt, and hand the file to a dedicated upscaling tool once the creative decisions are finished.

1.4 Managing jobs and downloads

Housekeeping that keeps a growing library usable: how jobs are identified, what you actually download, and how to avoid losing the prompt that made a picture you love.

The job record is the source of truth

In Discord, hovering or reacting to a message can surface its job id; in the web app every image carries its prompt and job id. Treat that record as the asset. If you download only the PNG, you have the pixels but not the recipe, and reconstructing a prompt from an image is guesswork. Save the prompt text alongside every file you keep — a plain text note or a filename suffix is enough.

Downloading

The web app offers a download button on any image, including its upscales, and Desktop or bulk exports exist for larger archives. Discord requires you to open an image and save it from the browser. Because the service is the canonical store, keep your own backups: subscriptions lapse, and an archive you no longer pay for is not a reliable long-term home for client work.

Do not rely on the Midjourney archive as your only copy. Download and back up anything you would be upset to lose. Account lapses, plan changes and service changes are all real risks to work that exists nowhere else.

2.1 The anatomy of a prompt

A Midjourney prompt is a description, not a command line. It reads like a compressed art brief, and the most effective prompts assemble a small, consistent set of ingredients in a predictable order. This section gives you that skeleton.

The seven ingredients

Almost every strong prompt specifies some combination of these:

  • Subject — who or what is in frame ("a roofless stone chapel").
  • Action or state — what the subject is doing ("overgrown with ivy and ferns").
  • Environment — where it sits ("on a clifftop above a grey sea").
  • Lighting — time and quality of light ("overcast dawn, diffused, cool tones").
  • Medium and style — how it is rendered ("watercolour sketch", "35mm photograph", "1970s travel poster").
  • Composition and camera — framing ("wide establishing shot", "low angle", "macro").
  • Parameters — machine instructions after the description (--ar 16:9 --stylize 400).
subject + action/state + environment + lighting + medium/style + composition --parameters

# filled in
roofless stone chapel, overgrown with ivy and ferns, clifftop above a grey sea,
overcast dawn, diffused cool light, 35mm photograph, wide establishing shot --ar 16:9 --stylize 400

Description versus instruction

Everything before the first -- is prose the model interprets; everything after is a parameter the software parses. Mixing the two breaks prompts: the word "aspect" is meaningful to a reader but meaningless to the model, whereas --ar 16:9 is an instruction with a precise effect. Write your intent as description and your technical requirements as parameters. When a prompt "ignores" you, it is usually because the requirement was phrased as description rather than a parameter.

Specific beats vague — but not always

Concrete nouns and named materials steer the model well ("oxidised copper", "wet cobblestone"), while abstract instructions ("make it beautiful", "high quality") mostly do nothing, because the model has no consistent notion of your taste. The counterweight is that too much specificity crowds the frame: dozens of adjectives compete for the same limited attention, and the model drops some at random. Six to fifteen meaningful words plus parameters is a reliable range to start from.

2.2 Word order, emphasis and weighting

Position and phrasing change how much the model cares about each idea. Ordering and weighting are the cheapest, most reliable levers you have, and they cost no extra GPU time.

Earlier words carry more weight

Midjourney reads prompts roughly left to right, and earlier tokens tend to dominate the composition. If the subject keeps getting lost behind a busy environment, move the subject to the front and the environment later. This is not an absolute rule — the model is not a strict parser — but it is a strong tendency you can exploit, and it is why "a red barn in a green field at sunset" and "at sunset, a green field with a red barn" produce recognisably different pictures.

Weighting syntax

You can nudge the importance of a concept by appending a multiplier. The syntax is a double colon followed by a number, applied to the word or phrase before it:

a cat::2 sitting on a windowsill::0.5, soft backlight

# cat is emphasised; windowsill is de-emphasised
# negative multipliers reduce further, but --no is clearer for removal

Weighting is a hint, not a dial with precise stops. Doubling a term does not literally double its influence; it makes the model more likely to preserve it. Use small adjustments — 1.5, 2 — and change one weight at a time, or you will not know which change did the work.

Repetition and phrasing

Because earlier and explicit mentions matter more, repetition can act as emphasis: naming "stormy sky" twice is a crude but sometimes effective way to push a mood. Prefer the weighting syntax and clear ordering first, though — repetition is easy to overdo and can skew a prompt toward a single element at the expense of everything else.

Reorder before you add. Moving the subject to the front often fixes a "where did my subject go" problem faster than bolting on more adjectives.

2.3 Negative concepts and what to leave out

Telling the model what you do not want is possible, but it is a weaker lever than telling it what you do want. Knowing the difference saves a lot of wasted generations.

The --no parameter

Append --no followed by the thing you want excluded, for example --no text or --no people. The model translates this internally into a negative prompt — a direction to move away from — rather than a hard filter. That distinction explains its behaviour: it reduces the likelihood of the concept appearing but cannot guarantee its absence, and excluding something the model strongly associates with your subject (asking for a beach but --no sand) fights the prompt and tends to produce worse images.

# prefer describing what you DO want, then exclude only the stubborn extras
a forest cabin at dusk, warm lit windows --no text, watermark, people

# fighting the prompt - expects sand, told not to draw it
beach panorama --no sand, sea, sky, people

Describe the absence, not the ban

When a negative fights the concept, positive phrasing usually wins. Instead of "a room --no furniture", describe "an empty room, bare floorboards". Instead of "--no text", name what the surface should be ("plain unmarked wall"). The model is far better at rendering a described positive than at enforcing an absence, so reach for negatives only for add-ons the model volunteers — watermarks, stray lettering, extra limbs — not for the core of the scene.

When negatives are genuinely useful

Negatives shine in cleanup: suppressing a recurring unwanted detail in a series, removing a style cue that a medium implies ("in the style of a photograph --no film grain"), or steering away from a version's habit. Keep the list short. A long --no list competes with your positive prompt for the same attention and can flatten the image.

Some concepts are restricted by the platform, not by your prompt. Certain names, likenesses and protected terms are blocked or altered by Midjourney's filters regardless of how you phrase them. Chapter 8 covers the policy side.

2.4 Describing light, lens and composition

Lighting and camera language are the highest-leverage vocabulary in Midjourney. A prompt that names its light looks intentional; one that does not looks generic. This section gives you the terms that actually change the picture.

Light as the mood engine

Lighting sets time of day, mood and colour temperature in one phrase. The model understands a large vocabulary of lighting terms, and small changes cascade: change "golden hour" to "overcast dawn" and the colour palette, contrast and shadows all shift. A practical list to have ready:

TermEffect
golden hour / blue hourwarm low sun / cool dusk
overcast, diffusedsoft, low-contrast, even
hard noon lightcrisp, high-contrast shadows
rim light, backlightsubject edges glow, separation from background
volumetric, god raysvisible beams through haze
neon / practical lightsnight-city colour, mixed sources

Lens and framing language

Photographic terms steer more than style: they change perspective and depth. Naming a focal length tells the model how compressed or wide the scene should feel, and a shot size tells it how much of the subject to include.

# shot size
extreme close-up, close-up, medium shot, full shot, wide establishing shot

# lens and depth
24mm wide angle, 50mm, 85mm portrait, 200mm telephoto compression
shallow depth of field, f/1.8 bokeh, deep focus, tilt-shift

# camera position
low angle looking up, high angle looking down, eye level, dutch tilt, aerial drone

Composition as instruction

You can direct the arrangement of the frame with a short list: "rule of thirds", "centred symmetrical composition", "leading lines", "negative space on the left for text". These work best when paired with a subject and a medium, and they are especially valuable when you need room for a headline or a product overlay later — asking for negative space up front saves a crop.

Build a personal palette of five lighting phrases and five lens phrases that you like, and reuse them. A consistent vocabulary across a project is what makes a set of images look like one body of work rather than random pulls.

3.1 Aspect ratio: --ar

Aspect ratio controls the shape of the canvas, and shape changes composition far more than beginners expect. This is the parameter to reach for first when an image feels cramped or empty.

Why ratio is a creative decision

Choosing --ar 16:9 versus --ar 1:1 is not just a crop. The model composes for the frame, so a wide ratio encourages landscape-like arrangements and horizons, while a square ratio pushes toward centred subjects. Changing the ratio changes the picture, not merely its border.

--ar 1:1     # square - social avatars, album art
--ar 3:2     # classic 35mm landscape/camera
--ar 16:9    # widescreen - video thumbnails, banners, film stills
--ar 9:16    # vertical - phone wallpaper, stories, reels
--ar 4:5     # portrait - Instagram feed
--ar 2:3     # taller portrait - posters, book covers
--ar 21:9    # ultra-wide - cinematic establishing shots

Ratios are relative, not absolute

Midjourney accepts a ratio, not a pixel size, and it caps how extreme the ratio can be. Very wide or very tall requests are clamped toward a supported range, so asking for --ar 1:10 does not give you a ribbon — it gives you the most extreme ratio the model supports. If you need a precise pixel canvas, generate in the nearest ratio and crop or extend in the Editor.

Ratio and outpainting together

If you already have an image in the wrong shape, do not regenerate it in a new ratio — the composition will change. Instead generate close, then use the Editor's pan and zoom (Chapter 5) to extend the canvas in the direction you need. This preserves the parts you liked and only invents new pixels at the edges.

Decide the destination before you prompt. A thumbnail needs --ar 16:9 at the moment of generation; asking for a square and cropping later throws away composition the model already gave you.

3.2 Stylize: --stylize / --s

Stylize is the single most important aesthetic dial. It trades literal prompt obedience for the model's own idea of beauty, and understanding that trade-off is what separates a competent prompt from a controlled one.

What the number means

Stylize sets the guidance strength, roughly between literal and artistic. Low values force the model to obey your words closely, producing plainer, sometimes awkward images. High values let the model's trained aesthetic take over, producing prettier images that may drift from what you asked. The default is 100, and the practical range is 0 to 1000.

ValueBehaviourUse when
0–50literal, unembellisheddiagrams, accurate description, faithful studies
100 (default)balancedgeneral work
400–600strongly stylised, polishedillustration, concept art, mood
800–1000theatrical, may ignore detailsexpressive, decorative, abstract-led images
# the same subject at three stylize strengths
a heron standing in reeds --ar 3:2 --stylize 20     # plain, literal
a heron standing in reeds --ar 3:2 --stylize 250    # polished, natural
a heron standing in reeds --ar 3:2 --stylize 800    # painterly, dramatic

The failure mode: high stylize hides mistakes

A very high stylize can make a wrong prompt look good. That is dangerous during exploration, because you may fall for an image that no longer matches your brief and cannot be steered back. When you are trying to hit a specific target, drop stylize, get the subject and composition correct at low values, then raise stylize once the bones are right.

Stylize and style raw

On current versions you can pair stylize with the --style raw modifier, which reduces the model's editorialising and yields a more photographic, less "Midjourney-looking" result. Raw plus a moderate stylize is a common recipe for images that need to look like photographs rather than illustrations. Chapter 4 covers raw and style references together.

3.3 Chaos and seed: --chaos / --c and --seed

Two parameters that govern variety and reproducibility. One explores, the other pins a result so it can be revisited, shared and iterated on exactly.

Chaos controls the spread

Chaos (--chaos, shorthand --c) ranges from 0 to 100 and controls how different the four images in a grid are from one another. At 0 the four are close siblings; at 100 they can be radically different interpretations. Chaos increases variety, not quality — it is a discovery tool, useful early in a project when you do not yet know what the image should be.

--chaos 0     # four near-identical siblings - great for fine-tuning
--chaos 25    # gentle spread - the common working default
--chaos 100   # four wild interpretations - brainstorming

Seed fixes the starting noise

A seed is a large integer that determines the random starting point. Reuse a seed with the same prompt and parameters and you get (approximately) the same images back, which is invaluable for A/B testing one change and for regenerating a lost result. Newer interfaces let you ask for a random seed and then read the value it chose, so you can capture a lucky one.

--seed 1234567890     # reproduce a previous generation
--seed random         # let the model pick, then note the number it reports

# disciplined A/B: hold everything constant, change one word
a fox in a birch forest, misty morning --seed 4200 --ar 4:5
a wolf in a birch forest, misty morning --seed 4200 --ar 4:5

Seeds are not portable across changes

A seed reproduces a result only when the prompt, parameters and model version all match. Change the version, the aspect ratio or even a word, and the seed produces a different image — a sibling in spirit, not a clone. Treat a seed as "the same starting dice roll", not as a saved file.

3.4 Quality, weird and tile

Three parameters that trade detail, novelty and seamlessness. Each is a specialist tool — knowing when not to use them is as valuable as knowing what they do.

--quality / --q

Quality controls how much rendering effort goes into an image, expressed as a fraction of a baseline in older versions (0.25, 0.5, 1, up to 2 or 4 depending on version). Higher quality costs more GPU time and adds fine detail; lower quality is cheaper and faster, good for drafts. In practice, quality changes surface detail rather than composition, so it is a late-stage knob — do not pay for quality while you are still deciding what the image should contain.

--q 0.25    # cheap draft, fast, low detail
--q 0.5     # mid draft
--q 1       # default
--q 2       # extra detail (version-dependent, costs more)

--weird / --w

Weird is a novelty control, 0 to 3000, that pushes the model toward unusual, unexpected results. Low values add a subtle oddness; high values produce genuinely strange, often abstract images. It is not a quality setting — more weird does not mean better, and it frequently means less coherent. Use it when you want an idea you would not have thought of, then dial it back for the polished version.

--tile

Tile makes the image seamlessly repeatable, for textiles, wallpapers, UI backgrounds and 3D texture maps. It costs nothing extra but changes what counts as success: judge a tile by whether the edges match, not by whether the scene is interesting. Generate a tile in a square ratio and test it by repeating it before you commit.

# seamless wallpaper: check that left/right and top/bottom edges meet
ornamental waves and koi, indigo and cream, flat vector --tile --ar 1:1

Not every parameter applies to every model version. Quality values, the maximum weird strength and whether tile is available all vary by version. If a parameter appears to do nothing, suspect the version before blaming your prompt.

3.5 Model versions: --version / --v

Midjourney ships successive trained models, and the version you pin is a creative choice, not an afterthought. This section explains how to choose one and why pinning matters.

Versions are different models

--v 7, --v 6, --v 5 and so on each select a distinct trained model with its own strengths in prompt adherence, realism, text rendering and aesthetic. Higher numbers are newer, not automatically better for your purpose: a version that renders hands and lettering well may be less painterly than an older one, and vice versa. The default is the newest version, so a prompt written for v6 will change subtly when the default moves to v7 unless you pin it.

--v 7     # newest default (subject to change over time)
--v 6     # prior generation, widely documented
--v 5.2   # older, distinctive look in some styles
--niji 6  # separate anime-focused model family (Chapter 4)

Pinning for a project

Once a project's look is right, put the version in every prompt. This makes the series consistent and protects you from a default change mid-project. Two images that differ only because the default version moved are a real and confusing failure mode; an explicit --v removes it.

Version-specific capabilities

Features like style references, character references and the fast draft path arrived in particular versions and do not work identically everywhere. When a tutorial's parameter does nothing for you, check the version first. Conversely, when you upgrade a version for one project, re-test your saved prompt templates, because the same words can read differently to a new model.

Record the version next to every prompt you save. "This prompt works" is incomplete advice without the model that made it work.

4 · Styles and aesthetics

4.1 Style raw versus default4.2 Style references: --sref4.3 The Niji model: --niji4.4 Aesthetic libraries and the style tuner

4.1 Style raw versus default

The --style raw modifier is a one-word change that shifts the whole character of the output. It is the fastest way to escape the recognisable "Midjourney look".

What raw does

By default the model applies a house aesthetic — a fondness for dramatic light, smooth gradients and a particular polish. --style raw reduces that editorialising and steers toward a plainer, more photographic result. Subjects render more literally, lighting is less theatrical, and the image reads more like a photograph than an illustration of one.

# default styling: polished, dramatic, "AI-looking"
portrait of a blacksmith, workshop, warm light --ar 4:5

# style raw: plainer, more photographic
portrait of a blacksmith, workshop, warm light --style raw --ar 4:5

When to use raw

Reach for raw when you need realism: documentary-style shots, product-adjacent imagery, editorial portraits, anything where the default gloss would look artificial. Avoid it when you want the painterly, decorative quality that makes Midjourney output distinctive — certain illustration and fantasy work benefits from the default style. Raw is not more correct; it is more neutral.

Combining raw with stylize

Raw and stylize work together. Raw removes the imposed aesthetic; stylize then controls how much of any aesthetic the model applies. A high stylize with raw produces a polished but non-formulaic image, which is a common target for professional work. Test the pair early, because their interaction is not obvious from the individual descriptions.

4.2 Style references: --sref

Style references let you point the model at an image and say "paint like this". They are the most powerful tool for keeping a body of work visually consistent, and they are worth mastering early.

The basic use

Append --sref followed by the URL of an image, or by a Midjourney style code — a short string you can save and share. The model extracts aesthetic qualities (colour, texture, rendering, mood) from the reference and applies them to your subject. The subject of the reference is not copied; only its look is.

# reference an image by URL
a mountain village at night --sref https://example.com/reference.jpg --ar 16:9

# reference a saved style code
a mountain village at night --sref 1234567890 --ar 16:9

# ask for a random style, then note the code it reports so you can reuse it
a mountain village at night --sref random

Style weight --sw

The style weight parameter, --sw (0–1000, default 100), controls how strongly the reference is applied. Low values let your prompt dominate; high values let the reference dominate and may overwhelm your subject. Tune it rather than treating style transfer as all-or-nothing.

Building a house style

A style code is the practical unit of consistency. Find or create a look you like, save its code, then prepend it to every prompt in a project. This is how a series of illustrations — a card game, a comic, a set of thumbnails — ends up looking like one artist made it rather than a random collection. You can combine multiple references to blend aesthetics, though the result becomes harder to predict as you add sources.

Keep a note with your favourite style codes and what each one looks like. A code is meaningless out of context, and a folder of saved codes with no labels is nearly useless a month later.

4.3 The Niji model: --niji

Niji is a separate model family tuned for anime and manga aesthetics. It is not simply "Midjourney in an anime style" — it is its own model with its own parameters.

What Niji is for

--niji selects a model trained specifically on anime and illustration, producing cleaner line art, more consistent character rendering and a stronger grasp of manga visual conventions than the main model achieves with style words alone. Aspects like cel shading, expressive eyes and dynamic action poses come out right far more reliably.

# Niji with one of its built-in styles
a swordswoman on a rooftop at night --niji 6 --style expressive --ar 3:4

# Niji default style
a quiet street after rain, warm shop lights --niji 6 --ar 16:9

Niji-specific style options

Niji exposes style modifiers such as expressive, cute and scenic, which shift the model's anime sub-genre. These do not exist on the main model, and the main model's --style raw does not apply to Niji in the same way. Treat the two model families as separate tools with separate parameter vocabularies.

Choosing between Niji and the main model

Use Niji when the target is genuinely anime, manga or a related illustrative style and you want that style's conventions honoured. Use the main model when you want an illustrative but non-anime look, or when mixing anime influence with photographic elements. Niji's specialisation is an advantage within its niche and a limitation outside it.

4.4 Aesthetic libraries and the style tuner

Beyond ad-hoc style codes, Midjourney offers tooling for exploring and saving aesthetics systematically. Using it well turns style from a lucky accident into a reusable asset.

The style tuner concept

The tuner presents pairs of sample images and asks you to choose preferences, then distils your choices into a style code. It is a guided way to discover the aesthetic you actually like, rather than guessing words and hoping. The output is the same kind of code you get from --sref, so the two tools compose.

Personalisation

Personalisation learns from the images you rate and upscale, building a profile the model can apply to new prompts. It biases output toward your demonstrated taste, which is powerful for consistency but can narrow your range if you rely on it exclusively — you will keep getting more of what you already made. Use it when you want a trademark look, and turn it off when you want to explore.

# apply a personalisation profile to a prompt (syntax evolves by version)
a deserted fairground at dawn --ar 21:9 --p

# save and reuse a discovered style
# 1) experiment, 2) capture the --sref code, 3) prepend it to the project's prompts

Curating a reference set

The most reliable "aesthetic library" is your own: a folder of ten to twenty images that share the look you want, each usable as an --sref. Because you chose them, you can describe the style in words and defend it to a client, which an opaque random code cannot do. Build the set deliberately and label each entry with the mood it produces.

Style references copy style, not content, and are intended for aesthetics you are entitled to use. Referencing a living artist's signature look or a brand's protected visual identity raises legal and ethical questions — see Chapter 8.

5 · Variations, upscaling and editing

5.1 The V and U buttons5.2 The Editor and inpainting5.3 Outpainting with pan and zoom5.4 Reroll and remix

5.1 The V and U buttons

The row of buttons under every grid is the main navigation of a generation. Knowing precisely what each does — and what it costs — makes iteration cheap and deliberate.

V makes variations

V1–V4 take one quadrant and generate four new images based on it, keeping its general character but changing details. This is the workhorse of refinement: you find a promising thumbnail, vary it, find a better variant, vary that, and walk the image toward what you want. Variations are new jobs costing GPU time, so drift is a budget as much as an aesthetic question.

U upscales

U1–U4 enlarge a quadrant. As noted in Chapter 1, current versions treat upscaling as a clean enlargement rather than a creative re-render, so upscale the thumbnail you already like instead of hoping upscaling will fix it. In some versions you are offered Subtle and Creative upscales; subtle stays faithful, creative adds new detail and can change the look.

# a typical refinement walk
1. grid of 4        -> pick the best quadrant
2. V3               -> 4 variations of quadrant 3
3. pick the best    -> V2 again (walk closer)
4. happy            -> U1 to enlarge the final pick

Cost and discipline

Each V press is a full generation of four images. Three rounds of varying is twelve images for one subject — perfectly reasonable, but it adds up across a project. The discipline is to vary the quadrant that is close and rewrite the prompt when none of the four is close. Vary when you are refining; rewrite when you are searching.

5.2 The Editor and inpainting

The web Editor lets you change part of a finished image without regenerating the whole thing. Inpainting is its core operation and the single biggest reason to prefer the web app over Discord.

Selecting a region

In the Editor you mask a region of the image — a hand, a sky, a logo, a face — type a prompt for what should replace it, and the model fills the masked area while keeping the rest of the image intact. This is inpainting. Because the surrounding pixels are fixed, the fill is contextual: a corrected hand matches the existing light and style far better than any full re-render would.

# inpainting workflow
1. open the image in the Editor
2. brush over the region to change (e.g. a misshapen hand)
3. type what belongs there: "faithfully drawn relaxed hand, same lighting"
4. generate, pick the best fill, apply

What inpainting is good for

Small, local corrections: fixing anatomy, removing a stray object, changing a colour, swapping a background element, replacing unwanted text. It is exactly the tool for the failure modes a text prompt cannot reliably fix, because you are pointing at the pixels rather than describing them.

Limits

Inpainting struggles when the region is large, when the change contradicts the surrounding image, or when the mask is careless. A big region gives the model little context and behaves like a partial re-render; a contradictory prompt fights the fixed pixels. Keep masks tight, describe the fill in the language of the existing image, and re-mask if a result is close but not right.

Describe the fill the way the original prompt described the scene — same lighting words, same medium — so the repaired region does not read as a patch.

5.3 Outpainting with pan and zoom

Outpainting extends an image beyond its original frame. It is how you change the shape of a finished picture, add breathing room, or build a wider scene without losing what you already have.

Pan and zoom

The Editor offers directional pan (extend the canvas left, right, up or down) and zoom out (extend in all directions). The model invents new pixels to fill the added area while keeping the original image unchanged. Panning once gives a wider shot; zooming out repeatedly creates a sense of pulling back, which can be used deliberately for a "reveal" effect.

# common outpaint uses
pan left    -> add headroom or lead room for a headline
zoom out    -> reveal a wider environment around a tight subject
pan up      -> extend a sky for a taller format
# combine: pan out twice, then inpaint details in the new areas

Why outpaint instead of regenerate

Regenerating in a new ratio changes the whole composition and abandons the image you liked. Outpainting preserves the parts that worked and only fabricates the new edges. For a finished piece that needs a different shape — a square image that must become a story-format vertical — this is the difference between keeping your work and starting over.

Watching the seams

New areas are invented with less context, so they can be blurrier, less detailed, or subtly off in lighting. Inspect the seam between original and extension, and use a little inpainting on any weak area once the dimensions are correct. The usual order is: get the shape right first, then fix the new edges.

5.4 Reroll and remix

Two final controls for re-rolling the dice and for editing a prompt in place. Both are small conveniences that nonetheless shape how you iterate.

Reroll

The reroll button re-runs the same prompt with new randomness, producing a fresh grid at the same settings. It is the honest way to explore a prompt's range without changing it: if four rerolls all miss, the prompt is wrong; if one hits, you have found a phrase worth keeping. Reroll costs a generation, so it is not free, but it is the correct tool for testing a prompt's reliability.

Remix mode

Remix lets you edit the prompt or parameters while generating a variation or upscale, rather than being locked to the original text. Turn it on in /settings and every V button becomes an editable prompt. This is a fast way to make targeted single-change experiments: keep everything, alter one word, and see the effect directly against the same seed.

# with remix on, pressing V3 opens the prompt for editing
# original:  ... a red rowboat on a calm lake --ar 3:2
# remixed:   ... a green rowboat on a calm lake --ar 3:2   # one word changed

Choosing your iteration tool

Reroll explores; vary refines; remix tests a hypothesis; the Editor repairs. Keeping those four jobs separate in your head stops the common trap of using reroll to fix a bad prompt, which only ever produces new bad images at the cost of a generation.

6 · Consistency and characters

6.1 Character reference: --cref6.2 Character weight: --cw6.3 Combining style and character references6.4 Seeds as a consistency tool

6.1 Character reference: --cref

Getting the same character to appear across many images is one of the hardest problems in generative art. The character-reference parameter is Midjourney's answer.

What --cref does

You supply one or more reference images of a character with --cref, and the model tries to preserve that character's appearance — face, hair, build, sometimes clothing — while your prompt determines the scene, pose and lighting. It solves the "same person, new situation" problem that pure text prompts cannot, because words alone cannot pin a specific face.

# one reference image
a young explorer reading a map by lantern light --cref https://example.com/me.png --ar 3:4

# multiple references (blend features)
a young explorer reading a map by lantern light --cref url1 url2 --ar 3:4

Best-practice references

Use a clear, well-lit, front-facing image with the subject filling much of the frame. A busy or low-resolution reference gives the model little to hold on to. A neutral expression, plain background and visible whole face yield the most consistent results. If a character varies too much between generations, the reference is usually the culprit, not the parameter.

Where character reference breaks

It is best-effort, not facial recognition. Extreme poses, heavy occlusion, dramatic lighting and style shifts all degrade fidelity, and the result is a likeness rather than an exact match. For a long series, lock the reference, the seed, the version and the style code together so the only thing changing is the scene — consistency comes from controlling all four, not from --cref alone.

Build a small "character sheet" first: three or four strong, consistent images of the character. Use the best as your --cref and keep the set as the project canon.

6.2 Character weight: --cw

Character weight decides how much of the reference is copied. It is the dial that separates "same face, new outfit" from "exact same character, outfit included".

What the number controls

--cw ranges from 0 to 100. At 100 (the default) the model references the face and the clothing and general look of the reference. At 0 it references the face only, leaving you free to dress the character however your prompt specifies. Intermediate values blend the two.

--cwReferencesUse when
100face + clothing + lookexact repeat of a full character design
50mostly face, some looksame person, slightly different styling
0face onlysame person in a brand-new outfit or uniform
# same face, wardrobe dictated by the prompt
a pilot on a flight deck, orange jumpsuit --cref url --cw 0 --ar 3:4

# exact same design including clothing
a pilot on a flight deck --cref url --cw 100 --ar 3:4

Why --cw matters for storytelling

Most series need the character to change clothes — a hero in armour in one panel and civilian dress in the next. Locking --cw 100 would refuse to change the outfit; dropping to 0 lets you write the wardrobe in the prompt while holding the face. Matching the weight to the story beat is what makes a cast of images feel like one character rather than a family of similar-looking people.

6.3 Combining style and character references

Style references fix the look; character references fix the person. Used together they give you a reusable cast inside a reusable aesthetic — the foundation of a real project rather than a set of one-offs.

The combination

Put both parameters in the same prompt: --sref for the visual treatment and --cref for the character, each with its own weight (--sw and --cw). The model applies the style to the subject and the character to the subject's appearance. This is how you generate a hundred images that all look like the same illustrator drew the same person.

a detective lighting a cigarette in a rainy alley
  --cref https://example.com/detective.png --cw 40
  --sref https://example.com/noir_palette.jpg --sw 200
  --ar 2:3 --style raw --stylize 250

Balancing the weights

Two references fight for influence. If the style is too weak, the character reads as pasted in; if it is too strong, faces drift from the reference. Start with a modest --sw and a mid --cw, then adjust one at a time. Because both parameters are guesses against a stochastic model, expect to run a grid or two before the balance settles.

Freezing a project recipe

Once the balance works, write the whole recipe down — version, style code, character reference, aspect ratio, stylize, and a sample prompt — and reuse it verbatim. A frozen recipe is the difference between a series and a pile. It also makes the project reproducible if you return to it weeks later.

Character references of real people raise consent and likeness issues. Reference images you have the right to use, and never build a likeness of a real, identifiable person without their permission — this is both a legal and a platform-policy matter. Chapter 8 covers it.

6.4 Seeds as a consistency tool

Seeds, introduced in Chapter 3, become a consistency technique once combined with references. Pinning the randomness removes one more source of variation from a project.

Seed plus reference

A seed alone gives you the same starting noise; combined with a character reference it stabilises the composition and lighting as well as the face. Reusing one seed across a set keeps framing and mood consistent, so the only difference between images is the content you deliberately changed. This is especially useful for storyboards and sequential art.

# same seed, same character, changing scene and action
--cref url --seed 7788 --ar 16:9   a knight entering a cathedral at dawn
--cref url --seed 7788 --ar 16:9   a knight kneeling before an altar
--cref url --seed 7788 --ar 16:9   a knight drawing a sword in the nave

When to break the seed

Seeds constrain as well as stabilise. If every image in your series shares an awkward composition because the seed dictates it, break the seed and re-establish the look with the style and character references instead. Consistency is a goal, not a rule — a varied set that reads as one project is better than a monotonous set that reads as one image.

Write down your seed, style code, character reference and version in a single project note. That note, not any single image, is the deliverable that lets you resume the project.

7 · Workflows and use cases

7.1 The iteration method7.2 Concept art and worldbuilding7.3 Product and marketing shots7.4 Thumbnails, covers and moodboards

7.1 The iteration method

A repeatable process beats inspiration. This section describes a way of working that reliably converges on a usable image without burning fast hours on random prompting.

Divergence, then convergence

Split the work into two phases. In divergence you prompt broadly, use higher chaos, and collect anything interesting without judging it hard — the goal is to find a direction. In convergence you freeze the strongest direction, drop chaos, and vary narrowly until it is finished. Most wasted effort comes from trying to judge and refine at the same time, which makes both worse.

# DIVERGE: wide, cheap, high chaos, vague prompts; collect directions
haunted forest, several moods --chaos 60 --q 0.5

# CONVERGE: freeze the winner, narrow, raise quality (when ready)
a moss-lit path between dead pines, fog, single shaft of moonlight,
dark teal and bone white, cinematic --ar 16:9 --chaos 0 --stylize 400

Change one thing at a time

When an image is close but not right, resist rewriting the prompt. Hold the seed and change exactly one element — one adjective, one parameter — so you can attribute the result. This is slow per step and far faster overall, because it builds a real mental model of what each word and parameter does. Random rewrites teach nothing because you never know which change mattered.

Keep a prompt log

Maintain a running note of prompts that worked, with their version and parameters. Prompts are reusable assets, and the log becomes your personal style library. Two weeks in, a good log is worth more than any collection of saved images, because it lets you reproduce and adapt rather than merely admire.

7.2 Concept art and worldbuilding

Midjourney excels at exploration: generating dozens of visual directions for a character, set or creature before any of them needs to be final. This is its strongest professional use.

Generating directions, not decisions

Concept work is about range. Prompt a subject with several environments, moods and eras to see what is possible, then narrow. The point is not a finished painting but a spread of options that lets a director or client point at one. High chaos and low quality are appropriate here because you are buying breadth, not polish.

# exploring a creature across biomes
armoured desert lizard, guild banner --ar 16:9 --chaos 40
armoured swamp lizard, bioluminescent plants --ar 16:9 --chaos 40
armoured arctic lizard, frozen tundra --ar 16:9 --chaos 40

Consistency for an art bible

Once a direction is chosen, build the "bible": style reference, character references, version and seed, all frozen. New assets — a vehicle, a location, a secondary character — are then generated inside that recipe, so the world coheres. The style reference does most of the heavy lifting for environment art; the character reference does it for the cast.

Hand-off to a human artist

Generated concept art is a starting point for a human, not a replacement. Its value is the speed with which it explores options and communicates intent; the final asset is usually redrawn, modelled or painted by a professional using the generated images as reference. Treat Midjourney as the first pass of a pipeline, not the last.

Generate a contact sheet on purpose: many variations at a low quality, arranged together, then present the grid. A wall of options communicates a direction far better than a single polished image.

7.3 Product and marketing shots

Product imagery is a demanding, high-stakes use case. Midjourney can produce striking marketing visuals, but the constraints are tighter and the failure modes more costly than in concept work.

Controlled staging

Product-type prompts reward control: fix the aspect ratio to the placement, name the lighting precisely, specify the surface and background, and keep stylize low to mid so the product is rendered faithfully. Style raw helps here, since a photographic look reads as more credible than an illustrated one.

a matte ceramic coffee cup, centred, soft studio light from the left,
seamless warm-grey background, subtle shadow, product photograph
--ar 4:5 --style raw --stylize 100

Text, logos and accuracy

The model invents lettering rather than setting real type, so any brand name, label or logo it draws will be subtly wrong — misspelled, mirrored or malformed. Never ship generated text as a brand asset. Generate the scene, then composite real typography and real logos in a design tool, where they will be crisp and correct.

Honesty and disclosure

Generated imagery used to advertise a real product can mislead if it implies a photograph of the actual item. Keep generated visuals to concept, mood and staging, and be transparent internally about which images are synthetic. Chapter 8 covers disclosure and platform rules for advertising.

Do not present a generated image as a photograph of a real product without clearly disclosing it. Competitor claims, consumer-protection rules and platform policies all apply, and a convincing synthetic product shot is exactly the case regulators are watching.

7.4 Thumbnails, covers and moodboards

Fast-turnaround formats — video thumbnails, article headers, album art, moodboards — are where Midjourney's speed pays off most. The trick is prompting for the layout you need, not just the picture.

Composing for text overlays

Ask for negative space and a simple background where the title will go. A busy, detailed image that looks great alone becomes unusable behind a headline. Specify the empty region and keep the subject to one side.

a lone astronaut on a dark ridge, subject on the right,
large empty dark sky on the left for a title, cinematic
--ar 16:9 --stylize 300

# then add the actual title in a design tool, not in the prompt

Covers and album art

Square and portrait ratios dominate here. Generate several directions, then refine one with style references so a series of covers or episodes shares an identity. The generated image sets the mood; real typography finishes the cover.

Moodboards

A moodboard is a set of images that communicates a direction — to a client, a team or yourself. Generate a consistent set using one style reference and a handful of related prompts, then assemble them into a single board. Because the images share an aesthetic, the board reads as intentional.

8 · Commercial use, ethics and copyright

8.1 What the licence allows8.2 Disclosure, likeness and protected styles8.3 Copyright of AI-generated work8.4 Responsible prompting

8.1 What the licence allows

Midjourney grants rights to its paid subscribers, but on terms that change and that carry conditions. This section explains the shape of the deal and how to stay on the right side of it.

Paid tiers grant asset ownership

Midjourney's terms grant paying subscribers rights to the assets they create, subject to the conditions in the terms of service. In broad terms, images you generate while subscribed are yours to use, on the conditions set by the applicable plan. Free tiers, where they exist, generally do not carry the same commercial rights. Read the current terms before you build a business on the output.

The revenue-threshold condition

A key condition in the terms is a revenue threshold: organisations above a certain annual revenue are expected to hold a higher-tier plan in order to use the images commercially. This is not a fee per image — it is a plan requirement. If your company exceeds the threshold, budget for the appropriate tier regardless of how few images you use.

Licence terms, prices and thresholds change and are governed by Midjourney's current Terms of Service, not by this guide. Always read the live terms before commercial use. If the stakes are high, get legal advice rather than relying on a summary.

The licence you grant Midjourney

The deal runs both ways. By generating, you grant Midjourney a broad licence to use and display your prompts and images — that is what makes the public gallery and the Explore feed possible. Public sharing is the default. If that is unacceptable for your work, you need a stealth-capable tier; otherwise assume anything you generate in a public channel may be shown to others.

8.2 Disclosure, likeness and protected styles

The rules of the road beyond the licence: honesty about synthetic media, consent for real people, and respect for others' identity and work. These are partly legal, partly ethical, and entirely your responsibility.

Disclosure

Several major platforms require that synthetic or AI-generated media be labelled, and audiences increasingly expect it. Disclosing that an image is AI-generated is usually cheap and always safer: it sets expectations, avoids accusations of deception, and increasingly matches platform policy. In advertising and journalism, non-disclosure of synthetic imagery can breach consumer-protection or editorial rules.

Likeness and real people

Generating a recognisable likeness of a real person — a celebrity, a colleague, a private individual — without consent raises rights-of-publicity, privacy and harassment concerns, and is frequently blocked by the platform as well. The rule is simple: do not build a likeness of a real, identifiable person without their clear permission. Reference images must be ones you have the right to use.

Protected styles and trademarks

Style itself is generally not copyrightable, but prompting in the style of a living artist, or in a company's protected visual identity, invites ethical criticism and, where trademarks or specific character designs are involved, legal risk. Naming an artist is also often filtered by the platform. The safe approach is to describe the qualities you want — "brutalist, high-contrast, limited palette" — rather than copying a name or a protected brand look.

# instead of copying a name, describe the attributes
# not:  in the style of <living artist>
# but:  high-contrast brutalist poster, limited two-colour palette,
#       heavy geometric shapes, screen-printed texture

8.3 Copyright of AI-generated work

Who owns a purely machine-generated image is a live legal question, and the answer varies by jurisdiction. This section states the general position honestly, including its uncertainty.

The human-authorship requirement

In the United States, the Copyright Office has held that works lacking human authorship are not copyrightable — meaning a purely prompt-generated image may not be protected. The reasoning is that copyright rewards human creativity, and a prompt alone has been treated as an instruction rather than authorship. Other jurisdictions are still developing their positions, and the law here is genuinely unsettled and moving.

What this means practically

If your image may not be protected, you cannot reliably stop someone else from reusing it, and it may be weaker as a registerable trademark or as the basis of an infringement claim. For commercial assets, the usual mitigations are: add substantial human authorship (arrangement, compositing, integration into a larger design), rely on other rights (trademark for a logo you actually use, contract and NDA for client work), and treat generated images as inputs rather than finished protected works.

Training-data controversy

Generative models are trained on large corpora of images, much of it scraped from the web, and the fairness and legality of that training is the subject of ongoing litigation and debate. Whatever the outcome, it is a reputational consideration for professional use: clients and audiences increasingly ask how an image was made, and an honest answer is part of the work.

This is a summary of a changing area, not legal advice. Laws, platform policies and Midjourney's own terms all evolve. For anything commercially significant, consult a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction.

8.4 Responsible prompting

A short, practical set of habits that keep your use of the tool defensible and your images safe to ship.

Practical habits

  • Keep the record. Save each prompt, version and the resulting file together, so you can attest to how an image was made.
  • Disclose by default. Label synthetic media unless there is a clear reason not to, and never claim a generated image is a photograph of something real.
  • Respect people. No likenesses of real, identifiable individuals without consent; no imagery that harasses, sexualises or defames.
  • Respect others' work. Describe qualities instead of copying named artists, brands or protected characters.
  • Mind the audience. Avoid generating content that is deceptive, harmful or illegal, and be aware that what you generate in a public channel is visible to others.
  • Use appropriately licensed references. Character and style references must be images you have the right to use.

When a client asks "is this AI?", the best answer is a short, confident one: yes, here is the prompt, here is the process, and here is the human work that turned it into the final asset. Transparency is easier to build in from the start than to retrofit.

9.1 Parameter cheat sheet

Every parameter covered in this guide, in one table, with its range and its effect. Ranges and availability vary by model version — check the official docs for the version you are using.

The parameters at a glance

Parameters are machine instructions placed after your prose and introduced by a double dash. Shorthands (--s, --c, --q) behave exactly like their long forms, so use whichever you find readable. Anything after the first -- is parsed by the software rather than interpreted as description, which is why a requirement phrased in words has no effect while the same requirement as a parameter does.

--ar 16:9        aspect ratio (shape of the canvas)
--stylize / -s   guidance strength, 0-1000; higher = more stylistic
--chaos / -c     variety within a grid, 0-100; higher = wider spread
--seed           fixes the starting noise for reproducibility
--no             negative prompt; exclude a concept
--quality / -q   rendering effort/detail (values version-dependent)
--weird / -w     novelty bias, 0-3000; higher = stranger
--version / -v   select the model version, e.g. --v 7
--tile           seamless repeatable image
--style raw      reduce the model's default aesthetic
--niji           anime-focused model family
--sref           style reference (image URL or style code)
--sw             style weight for --sref, 0-1000
--cref           character reference (one or more images)
--cw             character weight for --cref, 0-100
--repeat / -r    run the same prompt N times (tier-dependent)
--stop           stop early at a percentage (version-dependent)
GoalTry

Reading the table by intent

Work backwards from the result you want rather than memorising flags. If an image is too literal, raise --stylize; if it is too loose, lower it. If four thumbnails are nearly identical and you want more surprise, raise --chaos. If you cannot reproduce yesterday's image, you are missing a --seed or you changed the --v. Treat the "Try" column as the first experiment, not the only one.

9.2 Prompt templates

Copy-ready skeletons for the main use cases. Fill the brackets and delete what does not apply; keep the structure.

How to use a template

A template is a checklist of prompt ingredients, not a magic formula. Replace each bracketed slot with a concrete, specific phrase — concrete nouns and named materials steer the model far better than adjectives — then delete every slot that does not apply, because a crowded prompt makes the model drop elements at random. Keep the ingredient order: subject first, technical requirements last as parameters.

# Character portrait
[character], [action/expression], [environment],
[key light], [medium/film], [shot size] --ar 4:5 --style raw

# Landscape / establishing shot
[place], [weather/time], [key feature], [lighting],
[medium], wide establishing shot --ar 16:9 --stylize 300

# Product shot
[product], centred, [surface], [studio lighting],
[background], product photograph --ar 4:5 --style raw --stylize 100

# Thumbnail with title space
[subject], subject [left/right], large empty [sky/wall] opposite,
[lighting], cinematic --ar 16:9 --stylize 300

# Consistent series (freeze the recipe)
[scene] --cref [character-url] --cw 40
        --sref [style-url] --sw 200
        --v 7 --ar 3:4 --seed [fixed]

# Seamless texture
[pattern description], [palette], flat repeatable --tile --ar 1:1
