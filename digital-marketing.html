1 ▸ Orientation — Full-Stack Digital Marketing

1.1 What Full-Stack Digital Marketing Is1.2 The Funnel: TOFU, MOFU, BOFU1.3 Channel Overview and the Free Stack1.4 How This Guide Is Organised

Full-stack digital marketing means owning the whole journey — from a stranger's first impression to a customer's repeat purchase — by combining strategy, content, social, email, paid acquisition and analytics into one measurable system. This chapter explains what that system is, the funnel that orders it, the channels it runs on, and how this guide is organised.

1.1 What Full-Stack Digital Marketing Is

Full-stack is not a job title: it is a working model. Instead of mastering one tactic in isolation, you run a repeated loop of planning, building, distributing, measuring and iterating across every channel together.

One discipline, many channels

Digital marketing is a system, not a posting schedule. Each channel has a job: search captures demand that already exists, social creates demand for people who do not know you yet, email deepens the relationship after the click, and paid accelerates whichever of those you can measure. The discipline is choosing the right job for each channel, wiring them together, and letting the numbers decide where the next hour goes. Content you write but never distribute, an email list nobody was asked to join, and ads pointed at a page that does not match the ad's promise are each a broken link in the same chain.

Plan → Build → Distribute → Measure → Iterate
                              ↑
                              ┌──────────└─────────────┐
                              └──────────────────┐

Every activity in this guide fits into one of those five boxes. If an activity cannot be measured, it does not get a box; if it cannot be iterated, it is a one-off, not marketing.

The operating loop, applied

A concrete pass around the loop: you plan a target of 40 marketing-qualified leads, build a pillar article and a lead magnet, distribute through the newsletter and two social channels, measure signups with Google Analytics 4, then iterate by testing the lead magnet's headline. The point of the loop is that the iteration is cheap, repeatable and scheduled — never a scramble after a slow month.

Before any campaign, answer five questions:

1. Who is this for? ............. persona or segment name
2. What is the offer? ........... the one thing they get
3. Which channel? ............... where they already are
4. What is the KPI? ............. one number, checked weekly
5. What is the budget? .......... spend AND effort in hours

If you cannot answer question 4 with a single number, the campaign is not ready. "Traffic, engagement and maybe sales" is three numbers and no decision.

Sequence matters

Do not start with paid ads before you know your audience. Do not write content before you have a distribution channel. Do not build an email list before you have an offer worth subscribing to. Each impatient shortcut just charges the next problem forward: ads with no landing page leak money, content with no channel gathers dust, and a list nobody asked to join is a deliverability disaster. The order is deliberate: strategy first, distribution second, optimisation last.

The most common failure mode is not doing nothing — it is doing everything at once for two weeks, then stopping. A narrow, slow, consistent programme beats a broad, fast, dead one.

1.2 The Funnel: TOFU, MOFU, BOFU

The funnel is a model of how a stranger becomes a customer — and what happens after. It gives every piece of content, every email and every ad a stage to belong to, so you are never guessing what something is for.

The three stages and beyond

TOFU (top of funnel) is awareness: the stranger does not know you exist and is not shopping yet. MOFU (middle) is consideration: the person has a problem, knows it, and is comparing options. BOFU (bottom) is decision: the person is ready to act, and you need to remove the last risk. A modern funnel does not stop at purchase — loyalty and referral stages feed new strangers into the top, which is what turns a funnel into a growth loop (chapter 7 covers that in depth).

TOFU — blog posts, YouTube videos, infographics.
MOFU — comparison guides, case studies, webinars.
BOFU — free trial, consultation call, demo.

Example: a visitor reads a TOFU post on "what is SEO".
Next week they receive a MOFU email comparing SEO services.
The following week they book a BOFU consultation call.

Measure before you optimise

Every chapter points at a real metric: open rate, click-through, cost per acquisition, customer lifetime value, return on ad spend. Set up measurement first, run the campaign, and re-measure. Without data you are guessing, and guessing is not a strategy. Give each funnel stage its own KPI so you can see which stage is leaking: a funnel with plenty of traffic but no signups has a MOFU problem, not a traffic problem.

stage,action,kpi,benchmark
TOFU,first visit,sessions per week,500
MOFU,newsletter signup,list growth per week,40
BOFU,consultation booked,booking rate of signups,8%
LOYALTY,repeat order,repeat purchase rate,25%

Pick one primary KPI per channel and check it weekly. Opening every dashboard every day is a distraction, not a habit.

1.3 Channel Overview and the Free Stack

Channels are not interchangeable: each one has a job, a primary KPI and a cost profile. Match the channel to the funnel stage, then run it on a free stack until the numbers justify paying for tools.

The channel map

Search captures intent — someone is already looking, so your job is to be found. Social builds awareness and community where strangers live. Email and content deepen the relationship and convert it at near-zero marginal cost. Paid channels accelerate any of these but cost money per impression or per click, so they should only ever amplify a measured loop, never replace one. A small business should almost always start with the free channels and buy ads only once the funnel converts.

channel,job,primary KPI,cost profile
Search,capture existing demand,qualified sessions,pay-per-click
Social,build awareness and community,reach + engagement,effort
Email,deepen and retain,open rate + revenue,effort
Content,attract and educate,organic sessions,effort
Paid social,target cold audiences,CPM and CTR,pay-per-thousand

A typical free stack

You can run real marketing with free tools. Google Analytics 4 tracks traffic and conversions. Google Search Console shows search performance for nothing. Google Trends gives demand signals. Mailchimp's free tier sends email sequences. Meta Business Suite schedules organic posts and runs paid social. YouTube Studio reports video performance. All free, all sufficient for most small to mid-sized projects — and when you outgrow them, you will know exactly which paid feature you are buying.

Google Analytics 4      traffic and conversion tracking
Google Search Console   search performance, free
Google Trends           demand signals for topics
Mailchimp (free tier)   email sequences and forms
Meta Business Suite     organic scheduling + paid social
YouTube Studio          video performance and retention

Free tiers cap volume, not workflow. Build the habit on free tools so that paying for scale later changes nothing about how you work.

2 ▸ Strategy and Positioning

2.1 Goals and KPIs2.2 Target Audience and Personas2.3 Positioning and Messaging2.4 Budget and Channel Mix2.5 Planning Frameworks: SMART and OKR

A marketing strategy is a written plan: who you are targeting, what offer you are pushing, which channels you will use, and how you will measure success. Without it, every tactic is a guess. This chapter builds that plan in five steps — goals and KPIs, audience and personas, positioning and messaging, budget and channel mix, and the planning frameworks that keep it honest.

2.1 Goals and KPIs

Goals are outcomes; KPIs are the numbers that tell you whether you are reaching them. A goal without a KPI is a wish, and a KPI without a goal is a dashboard.

Choosing your KPI set

Choose one primary KPI per goal and give it four things: a precise definition (so two people cannot measure it two ways), a source (so it is verifiable), a baseline (so you know the starting point), and a target with a date. A KPI without a definition is useless — "leads" could mean form fills, phone calls, or handshakes, and the three numbers will disagree by an order of magnitude. Write the definition down; it is the single cheapest fix in marketing.

goal,KPI,source,target,owner,cadence
Grow pipeline,marketing-qualified leads,HubSpot lifecycle stage,40 per month,demand gen,weekly
Reduce cost,cost per acquisition,Google Ads conversions,under $60,paid lead,weekly
Improve retention,repeat purchase rate,order system cohort,25% within 60 days,lifecycle,monthly

Leading and lagging indicators

Lagging indicators — revenue, closed deals — tell you the outcome months late. Leading indicators — sessions, list growth, booking rate — change first and let you steer. A healthy dashboard has both: lagging numbers for the report to the owner, leading numbers for the weekly stand-up. If you only ever look at revenue, you will discover the problem three months after it started.

{
  "kpi": "marketing_qualified_leads",
  "definition": "form submit with budget AND timeline fields filled in",
  "source": "HubSpot lifecycle stage = marketing qualified lead",
  "baseline": 22,
  "target": 40,
  "owner": "demand-gen"
}

Never optimise to a vanity metric. A KPI you cannot attach a decision to — "impressions", "followers", "applause rate" — is decoration, not measurement.

2.2 Target Audience and Personas

The audience is the people most likely to buy; the persona is a written model of one of them. Both come from research, never from imagination. Talk to real people first, then generalise.

Interviews and jobs-to-be-done

Surveys scale and interviews go deep; do both, in that order. Start with ten to fifteen interviews, because a survey needs good questions, and only conversation shows you which questions are good. Jobs-to-be-done is the framing: people do not buy a product, they hire it to get a job done — "avoid a bad roof before winter". Ask about the last time they tried to get that job done, not about your product; that is where the truthful answers are.

1. Tell me about the last time you looked for [product/service].
2. What did you search for? Which websites did you visit?
3. What did you like or dislike about each option?
4. What would make you choose one over another?
5. If you could change one thing, what would it be?

Building a persona spec

A persona is a one-page summary of the customer: who they are, the job they are hiring you for, their frustrations, their trigger moments, the channels they actually use, and their objections. Keep it to one page — if it does not fit on a page, you have a novel, not a persona. Write the trigger moments specifically: "end-of-month revenue review" is a trigger; "sometimes" is not. The triggers tell you when your ads and emails should arrive.

# Persona: "Busy Beth" — service-business owner
Demographics: 34, owns a 6-person trades business, no marketing staff
Jobs to be done: a steady flow of qualified leads each month
Frustrations: wasted money on ads, no time to write, unclear ROI
Triggers: end-of-month revenue review, one bad review, a rival win
Channels: Facebook groups, YouTube at night, Google for tools
Objections: "is it worth it", "what if I sign up and it fails"
Proof needed: case study of a similar trade, 3-month pilot, fixed price

A persona invented in a room full of marketers is a mirror, not a customer. Every persona must trace back to at least three interview transcripts or, failing that, twenty customer-service tickets.

Voice of the customer

Voice of the customer (VoC) means using the exact words your audience uses in reviews, forums and support tickets. Their vocabulary — not your industry jargon — belongs in your headlines, ads and emails, because it is the language they already search with and trust. Collect it continuously: reviews are a free focus group at any scale.

// Amazon reviews for a similar product
"best value for the price"
"easy to set up, works straight out of the box"
"wish it had a longer cord"

// Your ad headline:
"The budget espresso machine that just works."

2.3 Positioning and Messaging

Positioning is how you describe your offer so the target person chooses you over the alternatives. Messaging is that positioning put into words that travel through ads, emails and landing pages.

ICP and the value proposition

ICP stands for Ideal Customer Profile — the specific person or company most likely to buy your offer. The value proposition is one sentence that says who gets what, how, and why it matters. The standard template forces the four parts into existence: the audience, the pain, the mechanism, and the benefit. If you cannot fill in all four slots, the offer itself is unfinished — and no copywriter can fix that.

For [target audience],
who [needs or pain],
our product [does what],
so that [benefit].

Example: "For local plumbers who lose leads to Google Ads, our service delivers qualified calls from organic search so that they stop paying per click." Note what the example does not say: no features, no "world-class", no adjectives that could describe any competitor.

Competitor teardowns

A competitor teardown forces you to look at how others market and where they leave gaps you can fill. Visit their site and note the first thing you see, run their pages through PageSpeed Insights, check which blog topics carry their traffic, and read their reviews to hear what customers praise and complain about. The gap is usually not "they are better" — it is a specific unserved need, like "no weekend appointments", that you can own in ten words.

1. Visit their website. What is the first thing you see?
2. Check their page speed with PageSpeed Insights.
3. Look at their blog: which topics get the most traffic?
4. Check their social media: which posts get the most engagement?
5. Read their reviews: what do people praise or complain about?
6. Note one thing they do well and one thing you could do better.

The messaging matrix

One positioning, many audiences. A messaging matrix maps each segment to its pain, its message and its proof, so the same campaign does not talk to a researcher and a ready-to-buy buyer in the same voice. The proof column is the part people skip — a claim without a proof is advertising; a claim with a proof is an argument, and arguments convert.

segment,pain,message,proof
New visitors,"don't know us","The budget option that actually works",review quote
Researchers,comparing options,"Compare us side by side, no jargon",comparison guide
Ready to buy,risking money,"30-day guarantee, fixed price",warranty text
Existing customers,waste in their stack,"Upgrade in one afternoon",support stats

Write the matrix in the customer's words from 2.2. If a message does not contain at least one phrase from a review or interview, rewrite it.

2.4 Budget and Channel Mix

Budget is only half the allocation problem — effort is the other half. Organic channels cost hours, paid channels cost money, and the mix has to be decided explicitly, then re-allocated on a schedule rather than on a mood.

Effort versus spend

Write the mix down with percentages for both effort and spend, because they follow different rules. Content and email cost effort and compound; paid costs cash and stops the moment you stop. A sane starting point for a product or service without a big war chest is effort-heavy: most hours into organic content and email, a minority of cash into one paid channel once the funnel converts. The one-page strategy template below is the deliverable — if it fits on a page, everyone can agree on it.

# Marketing Strategy — Q4 2026

## Target audience
Small business owners, 25–45, who sell services locally.

## Core offer
A 12-week SEO growth plan at $800/month.

## Channels — effort and spend
1. Organic content (blog + YouTube) — 40% effort, 10% spend
2. Email newsletter — 30% effort, 5% spend
3. Local sponsorships — 20% effort, 25% spend
4. Paid search — 10% effort, 60% spend

## Primary KPI
50 qualified leads per month, measured in HubSpot.

## Budget
$2,000/month. 60% ads, 25% tools, 15% content production.

Re-allocating by rule

Do not re-allocate every time a dashboard dips — you will chase noise. Re-allocate once a month, by a written rule: take 10% from the channels under target and give it to the over-performer, capped so no channel doubles in one step. Organic channels get a 90-day judgement window, because content and email compound on a longer clock; judging a blog on week two is judging a tree by its first leaf.

Reallocate once a month, by rule, not by gut:

1. For each paid channel: ROAS over the last 4 weeks and its trend.
2. Take 10% from underperformers (ROAS below target).
3. Give it to the best performer, capped at +20% in one step.
4. Leave organic effort alone for 90 days before judging it.
5. Record the decision and the reason in the monthly report.

Cutting a channel that feeds another can starve the winner. Retargeting often looks tiny until you stop it and watch awareness-driven sales vanish — check dependencies before you cut.

2.5 Planning Frameworks: SMART and OKR

SMART makes individual goals verifiable; OKRs connect company ambition to marketing work and force a weekly conversation about progress. Use both: SMART for the KPI, OKR for the quarter.

SMART goals

SMART is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. The value is in what it refuses: "grow leads" passes no test and commits nobody. The trick is that the measurable part must name the instrument — "measured in HubSpot" — so the target is auditable, and numbers must come with a baseline or "raise from 22 to 40" means nothing. Write the before and after next to each other for the full effect.

Before: "Get more leads."

After:  "Raise monthly marketing-qualified leads from 22 to 40
         by 31 March, using SEO and email only, at a cost per
         acquisition under $60, measured in HubSpot."

Objectives and key results

An OKR pairs one qualitative objective with three to five quantitative key results. The objective should embarrass you a little; the key results should have numbers and sources. The discipline is the weekly check-in: each key result gets a status line, and the team answers stop, continue or accelerate. Marketing OKRs fail in two standard ways — objectives that are really tasks ("launch the blog" is not an outcome), and key results nobody can verify. Test every KR with the question "how would we prove this next Monday?"

Q4 Objective: Build a repeatable lead engine for the sales team.
  KR1: 40 MQLs/month from organic + email (rolling 28-day count)
  KR2: welcome sequence open rate above 60%
  KR3: content library covers all 5 buyer questions (scored checklist)
  KR4: lead-to-demo conversion holds above 25%

Weekly check-in format:
  KR1: 33/40   KR2: 58%   KR3: 3/5   KR4: 27%
  Decision: stop, continue, or accelerate — one word per KR.

3 ▸ Content Marketing

3.1 Content Strategy3.2 Content Formats3.3 SEO-Aligned Content3.4 Distribution and Promotion3.5 Content Calendars and Measurement

Content is the asset every other channel spends: it feeds search, fills the newsletter, gives social something to share, and gives ads a landing page that converts. This chapter covers the strategy, the formats, SEO alignment, distribution, and the calendar and measurement that keep it honest.

3.1 Content Strategy

Before producing anything, write the strategy: who the content is for, what job it does in the funnel, and what it must achieve. A strategy is three paragraphs that stop a thousand bad articles.

The editorial mission

An editorial mission is one sentence with four parts: audience, outcome, method and proof of difference. It is the filter every pitch goes through — if a topic does not serve the mission, it does not get made, no matter how catchy. Teams without a mission drift toward producing what is easiest to write this week, which is how a plumbing blog ends up publishing recipes. The mission makes the "no" easy.

Our content exists to help [audience]
achieve [outcome],
using [method],
in a way that proves [differentiator].

Example: help local trades businesses get a steady flow of
leads using SEO and email, with receipts, not hype.

Pillars and clusters

The pillar-and-cluster model builds topical authority: one long pillar page answers the big question comprehensively, and cluster posts cover the sub-questions, each linking up to the pillar while the pillar links down to them. The cluster keeps every page focused — a 2,000-word pillar that tries to cover twelve sub-topics satisfies nobody — and the internal links tell search engines you own the topic, not just a page. Start with three pillars a year, not thirty posts a month.

Pillar:  "The complete guide to local SEO for trades" (long, converts)
Cluster posts (each links up to the pillar, pillar links down):
  - "Google Business Profile: 12 settings to check"
  - "Reviews: how to ask, and where to respond"
  - "Local landing pages: one per service area"
  - "Citations: which directories matter in 2026"

One pillar with four cluster posts beats a hundred orphaned articles. Orphans have nothing to rank through and nobody to pass authority to.

3.2 Content Formats

Format is a strategic choice, not a taste: it must match the funnel stage and the channel the audience actually uses. Choose the format for the job, then let one long asset spawn many short ones.

Matching format to stage

Different jobs take different formats. Short video wins the top of the funnel because reach and recall are its whole game; comparison guides and case studies carry the middle because choosing needs evidence; the bottom wants a demo, a consultation or a risk-reversed offer. The effort column matters too — a webinar is five times the production cost of a social post, so spend the expensive formats where the funnel converts rather than where it merely entertains.

format,funnel stage,best for,effort
Blog post,TOFU,answering questions,medium
Comparison guide,MOFU,helping the reader choose,high
Case study,BOFU,proof and objection handling,high
Short video,TOFU,reach and brand recall,low
PDF lead magnet,MOFU,email list growth,medium
Webinar,BOFU,demo and live Q&A,very high

Repurposing: one asset, five outputs

Production is the expensive part, not distribution — so amortise it. One 2,000-word pillar becomes a video script, four social posts, an email issue, a carousel and three pull quotes, each pointing back to the pillar. The rule of thirds keeps the pipeline honest: a third new, a third repurposed, a third curated. A repurposing pipeline also gives the social calendar a reliable source, so you are never staring at a blank week.

One 2,000-word pillar post becomes:
  - 1 short video (script from section one)
  - 4 social posts (one per section: quote + link)
  - 1 email issue (summary + link back)
  - 1 carousel (top-five takeaways)
  - 3 newsletter pull quotes

Rule of thirds: 1/3 new, 1/3 repurposed, 1/3 curated.

3.3 SEO-Aligned Content

SEO alignment is writing for the query a person actually searched, not just for the topic. Intent beats volume; on-page basics are mechanical and checkable. This is an overview — the library's website-seo guide carries the full depth.

Intent before volume

A keyword is a question with an intent attached: informational ("how to …"), commercial ("best …"), or transactional ("buy …"). Search volume tells you how many people ask; intent tells you what they will do with your answer. Ranking a how-to article for a buying query can tank a page's conversion rate — the searcher gets educated, then leaves. Before writing, search the keyword yourself and look at what format ranks: if the top results are videos and buying guides, a text tutorial is fighting the SERP, not the competition.

keyword,intent,volume,difficulty,winner format,angle
"espresso machine for home",informational,5400,62,video + buying guide,ranking factors
"best budget espresso machine",commercial,2900,48,buying guide,price bands
"buy espresso machine online",transactional,720,35,product pages,trust signals

Volume without intent is a trap: a 5,000-volume keyword that never converts is worth less than a 300-volume keyword that does. Optimise for the money keyword, not the biggest one.

On-page essentials

The mechanical layer is small and checkable: a unique title tag under about 60 characters, a description of 148–156 characters that states the promise, one H1, a URL that reads as a word, and internal links both in and out. The title should contain the keyword near the front and a reason to click — "2026 Tested" beats "Guide" every time. Do not duplicate a title across two pages; search engines then decide for you which one is canonical, and it may not be the one you wanted.

<title>The Budget Espresso Machine That Just Works (2026 Tested)</title>
<meta name="description" content="We tested 12 machines under $500. See the
three that pull a real shot — with a 30-day money-back guarantee.">

h1:  The 3 Best Budget Espresso Machines, Tested
URL pattern: /best-budget-espresso-machine
Internal links: one in from /how-to-pull-a-shot, one out to /espresso-machines

3.4 Distribution and Promotion

Content does not distribute itself. Publishing is the start of distribution, and paid amplification should only push pieces that already perform organically. A distribution playbook turns "post it somewhere" into a timed sequence.

Owned, earned and paid

Owned channels are yours — the blog, the newsletter, the YouTube channel. Earned is other people carrying your work — links, shares, guest appearances, community answers. Paid accelerates either with money. The sequence that works: publish on owned, earn attention in the communities where the audience lives (by answering questions, not dropping links), and only pay to amplify something that already earned traction without money. Amplifying a dud just makes a dud visible to more people.

For every published piece, run this list:

Day 0: post to owned channels (blog, newsletter, YouTube, LinkedIn)
Day 0: answer 3 related community questions, link only when useful
Day 3: repost with a new angle for a different audience
Week 2: measure; if a winner, syndicate to the second platform
Month 1: fold evergreen pieces into the email welcome sequence

The 80/20 rule applies: most pieces earn little, a few earn everything. Distribution effort belongs on the winners, and the only way to know the winners is to measure every piece the same way.

Syndication rules

Syndication — republishing elsewhere — is safe if you keep one rule: your domain publishes first as the source of truth, and every cross-post carries a canonical link back to it. Browsing syndication by eye, search engines read canonicals; get them wrong and you compete against yourself. Community link policy deserves its own rule: value first, links second. The accounts that get muted on Reddit and in forums are the ones treating every answer as a billboard.

1. Publish on your own domain FIRST (the source of truth).
2. Cross-posts follow, each with rel=canonical pointing home.
3. Never duplicate inside your own site — one URL per topic.
4. Pick the audience's prime time, not your own timezone's.
5. Link policy: value first, links second — spam gets you muted.

3.5 Content Calendars and Measurement

A calendar turns strategy into a schedule; measurement turns output into learning. The calendar is a plan, and the scorecard is the verdict — both are plain documented artifacts.

The content calendar

Put every piece on one calendar with the same columns: date, type, topic, format, channel, owner and status. The discipline is that the calendar is a plan, not a promise — move pieces rather than deleting them, so no work silently evaporates. Keep status honest: an "idea" is not a "draft", and a "draft" is not "published". If the calendar has no owners, it has no accountability; every row needs exactly one name.

date,type,topic,format,channel,owner,status
2026-10-12,blog,"10 Google Business Profile settings",post,SEO,ana,draft
2026-10-13,social,"review-ask tip of the week",carousel,LinkedIn,ben,scheduled
2026-10-14,email,"This week in local SEO",newsletter,email,ana,scheduled
2026-10-15,blog,"Local landing pages: one per area",post,SEO,ana,idea
2026-10-16,video,"Pillar recap: local SEO checklist",YouTube,ben,idea

The content scorecard

Measure every piece with the same columns — views, signups, leads, conversion — so a monthly scorecard sorts winners from noise in one glance. The verdict column is the whole point: repurpose the winners, renew what is close, and cut what is genuinely thin. Repeat a piece's data monthly; a good article compounds for a year, and the scorecard is what proves it instead of trusting the feeling.

4 ▸ Social Media Marketing

4.1 Channel Selection4.2 Organic Strategy4.3 Community Management4.4 Content Cadence4.5 Social Listening

Social media marketing is the awareness and community layer of the system: it puts the brand where the audience already spends time, proves the offer with people, and feeds the funnel from the top. This chapter covers choosing channels, the organic strategy, community management, cadence, and listening.

4.1 Channel Selection

Pick channels where the audience already spends time and where the format fits the brand — not where the brand wishes it was. Two strong channels beat six neglected ones.

The fit matrix

Score every candidate channel on audience overlap, format fit and the effort per week it genuinely demands, then decide. The matrix's job is to make the "skip" explicit: a channel with poor audience overlap is a hobby, not a strategy, no matter how trendy. For a local trades business, Facebook groups and local community pages routinely out-earn everything else because the buying decision is local and conversational; for a B2B software company the same budget belongs on LinkedIn. There is no universal winner — only fit.

channel,audience overlap,format fit,effort/week,verdict
LinkedIn,40%,B2B posts,4h,"strong for B2B — start here"
Instagram,70%,visual,6h,medium
TikTok,30%,short video,8h,skip until resourced
Facebook,60%,groups + comments,4h,strong for local trades
YouTube,50%,long video,10h,second wave

A channel hypothesis

Treat every channel as an experiment with a written hypothesis, a test window and kill criteria — the same discipline chapter 7 applies to landing pages. "We post on Instagram" is not a plan; "3 posts a week in Facebook groups will bring 20 qualified conversations a month, and we stop if it delivers fewer than 8 with zero booked calls" is. The kill criteria matter most: they are what let you drop a failing channel without a debate in six weeks, and keep the budget for the channel that is actually working.

Hypothesis: our local-trades audience spends weekday evenings in
Facebook groups, so 3 posts a week there will bring 20 qualified
conversations per month into the DMs.

Test window: 6 weeks.
Success: 20 conversations, 5 booked calls.
Kill criteria: fewer than 8 conversations, or zero booked calls.

4.2 Organic Strategy

Organic social needs the same structure as content: pillars that say what you post, a profile that converts, and a cadence you can actually sustain. Structure is what separates a channel from a feed.

Content pillars

Content pillars are the buckets every post falls into, with explicit percentages so the mix cannot drift. A credible mix for a services brand is roughly 40% teaching, 20% proof (before/after jobs), 20% people (team, process — the trust builders), 10% pure selling, and 10% reacting to industry news and local events. The percentages do the governance: if the last ten posts are all offers, the pillar percentages prove it and reset it. No pillar percentages means whatever is easiest this week wins.

Pillar mix for a trades brand (per quarter):
  - 40% teach: "how to check your roof before winter"
  - 20% prove: before/after job photos with captions
  - 20% people: team, tools, process — trust builders
  - 10% sell: service explainers, offers, booking steps
  - 10% react: industry news, tool launches, local events

A profile that converts

The profile is the landing page of the channel: nearly every visit that matters arrives at the bio before anything else. Rewrite it as a three-line pitch — who you are, the proof, and the one action — and point the single link at one destination, never a link tree full of choices. "One link, one action" is the rule; every extra click between the profile and the booking page is a conversion leaked. Change the linked destination when the campaign changes: during a launch, the one link points at the launch page, and nowhere else.

Before: "We do roofs. Call us."

After:  "Roofing that survives the storm — before/after on the
feed. Free 30-point roof check, book in under 2 minutes.
Serving the metro area since 2012."

Linked bio link: ONE destination — the booking page.

Treat the bio like an ad headline: it gets rewritten for every campaign, and the link changes with it. A static bio is a static offer.

4.3 Community Management

Community management is the job of answering, moderating and escalating — with a process, not a personality. Response times and tones are written down so they survive whoever is on shift.

The response SLA

An SLA (service level agreement) turns "we reply quickly" into numbers per channel: DMs within two working hours, comments within a day, reviews within 48 hours, and complaints escalated within the hour. The first reply is a qualification move, not a sales pitch: one question that moves the conversation forward beats three paragraphs of brochure. Canned openers are fine — they are not lazy, they are consistent — but they must ask something specific, never "any questions?".

channel,first response,goal,canned opener
DMs,under 2 working hours,"two questions to qualify","Thanks for reaching out — where are you based?"
Comments,under 24h,answer or one-line ack,"Good question — short version: ..."
Reviews,under 48h,thank + fix in private,"Thanks for the feedback — may we call you?"
Complaints,under 1h,de-escalate + move private,"Sorry about this — can we talk briefly by phone?"

Moderation and user content

Moderation rules protect the community and the brand's legal position. Publish a short ruleset: spam links, slurs and personal attacks are hidden or removed; the first genuine complaint is answered, not deleted — deleting criticism is how a one-star complaint becomes a five-post saga. User-generated content (UGC) is gold, but reposting someone's photo without asking is a rights problem: ask permission in the thread before reposting, and keep the consent note in the files. Log tone violations and escalate repeat offenders to the owner; nobody should decide policy mid-argument at 11pm.

1. Block-and-hide list: spam links, slurs, personal attacks.
2. Respond, do not delete, the first genuine complaint.
3. Fake reviews: reply once on the record, then flag the platform.
4. UGC reuse: ask permission in the comment thread BEFORE reposting.
5. Log tone violations; escalate repeat offenders to the owner.

Never delete a genuine complaint. A public "we are sorry, here is the fix" converts more observers than a deleted post ever will — and a deleted post guarantees a screenshot.

4.4 Content Cadence

Cadence is consistency over intensity: three posts a week for six months beats ten a day for two weeks. A schedule you can keep is the deliverable, and batching is what keeps it.

A sustainable weekly schedule

Pick a frequency and pin it to a schedule with named posts and days, then leave room for community time — the replies and conversations that actually build the audience. A realistic week for a small team is three to four posts plus daily reply windows: enough for the algorithm to take you seriously, light enough to survive a busy month. Schedule the community time as a block, not as "when I get a minute"; if replies never happen, the posts are billboards, not a community.

Monday    09:00  teach post (Facebook + LinkedIn)
Tuesday   12:00  community block: answer 5 questions in groups
Wednesday 09:00  prove post (before/after + story)
Thursday  12:00  community block + batch filming for next week
Friday    16:00  people post (team / process)
Saturday  —    replies only, no new posting

Batching beats daily drafting

Drafting in the moment is how schedules die — the feed goes quiet the first week you are busy. Batch instead: one two-hour block a week produces seven days of posts, because captions get written in one voice, visuals are collected in one pass, and the scheduler is set once. Keep the daily budget to replies only. The batching checklist is the anti-burnout artifact: writing, shooting, scheduling, and twenty minutes of daily community time on top.

One batch day (2 hours) a week covers 7 days of posting:
1. Write all captions in one doc, one voice per channel. (45 min)
2. Shoot or collect all visuals — 3 photos per post minimum. (30 min)
3. Schedule everything; check the calendar for conflicts. (20 min)
4. Leave 20 minutes a day for replies ONLY, never drafting. (daily)

4.5 Social Listening

Listening means watching what is said about the brand, the category and the pain — outside the follower count. Mentions are a research feed, and a weekly listening pass turns noise into next week's content and fixes.

Queries and sources

Set up a fixed query set covering four layers: the brand name (including misspellings, which catch the gripes people post when they are cross), the product or service, the category and the pain, and the competitors. Spread the queries across sources — platform search, Reddit, Facebook groups, Google Alerts, review-site pulls — and run the pass weekly on a timer, twenty minutes, no exceptions. The queries are the same every week so the report is comparable; changing the queries changes the baseline and the trend disappears.

Brand: "brandname", "brandname reviews", misspelling variants
Product: "our product name", "our service + your city"
Category: "roof repairs metro", "storm damage claims"
Pain: "roofer too expensive", "leak repair quote"
Competitors: "competitor A", "competitor B"

Sources: platform search, Reddit, Facebook groups,
Google Alerts, review pulls — weekly, 20 minutes.

From mentions to insight

A listening report is short and actionable: mention count and trend, one emerging complaint, one praise-worthy moment, one opportunity, and the action for this week. The value is the action column — if a report never produces an action, the listening pass is a hobby. Repeated complaints ("no weekend appointments" nine times) are a product or policy change, not a copywriting problem; the fastest win is often fixing the thing the audience keeps saying, then telling them it is fixed.

Period: 2026-10-05 to 2026-10-11
Mentions: 34 (up 6), 1 crisis, 3 praise-worthy
Top complaint: "no weekend appointments" (9 mentions)

+ ship one reply template for the quote request

Listening output feeds everything upstream: the complaints become 2.2's voice of the customer, the questions become 3.1's editorial mission, and the quote requests justify the ad budget in chapter 6.

5 ▸ Email and Lifecycle Marketing

5.1 List Building and Hygiene5.2 Segmentation5.3 Automations and Sequences5.4 Deliverability5.5 Subject Lines, A/B Testing and Compliance

Email is the highest-leverage channel in the stack: it is owned (no algorithm in the middle), measured open by open, and cheap at scale. This chapter covers building the list, segmenting it, automating the lifecycle, protecting deliverability, and doing it legally — with honest subject lines and consent.

5.1 List Building and Hygiene

A list is an asset only if the people on it gave permission and the addresses stay valid. Build it with specific offers, verify permission with double opt-in, and prune it on a schedule.

Lead magnets and forms

A lead magnet is a specific deliverable exchanged for an address — a checklist, a template, a guide, a calculator — and the specificity is the whole trick: "tips" does not buy an email address, "the 12 settings your Google Business Profile is missing" does. Keep the form to the minimum (email plus one qualifier), because every field after the first costs signups. The consent checkbox is not cosmetic — it is the legal record that permission was given, and the wording matters more than copywriters think.

<form action="/subscribe" method="POST">
  <label>Business email
    <input type="email" name="email" required>
  </label>
  <label class="consent">
    <input type="checkbox" name="consent" required>
    Send me the weekly SEO tip sheet. Unsubscribe anytime.
  </label>
  <button type="submit">Get the tips</button>
</form>

Double opt-in and hygiene

Double opt-in means the signup triggers a confirmation email and the address is only added when the subscriber clicks it. It costs a few signups and buys you a list of people who actually want the mail — which is exactly the population that protects your deliverability. Record the consent event (document version, timestamp, IP) so you can prove permission if you are ever asked to. Hygiene is the other half: purge hard bounces immediately, and sunset contacts who have not opened in 90–180 days rather than mailing them for ever, because a list full of dead addresses is how reputations rot.

{
  "subscriber": "beth@example.com",
  "signup": "2026-10-11T09:12:00Z",
  "source": "blog: gbp-guide",
  "opt_in": "double",
  "consent_doc": "weekly-tips",
  "consent_version": "2026-01",
  "ip": "86.12.44.201",
  "gdpr_region": true
}

Buying or scraping lists is a deliverability death spiral: high bounces and complaints get your domain blocked at the mailbox providers, and the block follows the domain, not the campaign. Build your own list or do not send.

5.2 Segmentation

A segment is a group defined by data — signup date, behaviour, purchase history. Segmentation is what makes email feel personal without a single personalised hand-written line.

Defining segments

Define segments by a rule and a use, and keep the definition written down so the list means the same thing next quarter. Rule-based segmentation beats this month's campaign idea: a "new subscribers" segment is anything under 14 days old and gets the welcome and first taste; "engaged readers" opened two of the last four campaigns and gets the weekly digest; "at-risk" has not opened in 90 days and gets the re-engagement or sunset sequence. Segments are operational infrastructure, not decoration — the campaign uses them, never the other way round.

segment,name,rule,use
s1,new subscribers,signup less than 14 days ago,welcome + first taste
s2,engaged readers,opened 2 of last 4 campaigns,weekly digest
s3,at-risk,no open in 90 days,re-engagement or sunset
s4,hot leads,visited pricing AND submitted a form,premium offer
s5,repeat buyers,2 or more orders,advocacy + referral ask

RFM in practice

RFM scores every subscriber on recency, frequency and monetary value so effort goes where the value is. The recency ladder makes it concrete: bought or opened this week scores 5 and gets immediate attention; cold for 180+ days scores 1 and should be purged, not mailed. The rule that matters is the one nobody wants: never mail the score-1s. One final sunset message, then delete — retention theatre on a dead address only drags average open rates down and feeds spam filters.

score,recency window,meaning,action
5,0-7 days,"bought or opened this week",immediate follow-up offer
4,8-30 days,recent but not this week,relevant content
3,31-90 days,softening,reactivation nudge
2,91-180 days,cold,win-back series
1,180+ days,gone,purge at the next hygiene pass

Rule: never mail the 1s; delete them after the final sunset.

5.3 Automations and Sequences

Automation sends one message at the moment it matters — triggered by behaviour, not by the calendar. Map the journey first, write the copy second, and let branches follow what the subscriber does.

Lifecycle journeys

A lifecycle map is the series of automated journeys: welcome, nurture, abandoned cart, post-purchase, win-back. Each journey is a trigger plus messages plus branch rules — the branch is what makes it feel intelligent ("if the subscriber viewed pricing, jump to the premium track"). Map the trigger before touching the copy: the trigger defines the moment, and the moment defines the relevance. A welcome sequence that arrives while the reader still has the guide open converts; the same sequence three weeks later is spam.

Trigger: form submit on /gbp-guide
  → Welcome 1 (day 0): deliver the guide, set expectations
  → Welcome 2 (day 2): tip worth saving + proof
  → Welcome 3 (day 5): common mistake + the fix
  → Nurture 4 (day 10): case study, objection handling
  → Conversion 5 (day 14): free consultation, expiry date

Branch: clicked "pricing" at any step → jump to the premium track
Branch: no open by day 7 → plain-text variant from day 8

A welcome sequence with goals

Give every message in the sequence a job and rotate the subject lines so the sequence learns as it runs. Day 0 delivers the promise — the guide itself — because killing the expectation is the fastest way to kill the relationship. Day 5 exists to apply the advice, day 10 to handle objections with a story, day 14 to convert. A sequence without goals degenerates into five pleasant emails; a sequence with goals has a verdict after 14 days, which is precisely what the next iteration needs.

day,subject (rotating A/B),goal
0,"Your free guide — and how to beat the default setup",deliver + prove
2,"The 3 settings most businesses get wrong",save + share
5,"The mistake that hides you from Google (fix in 4 min)",apply
10,"How a 6-person team filled their diary in 6 weeks",objections
14,"A free 30-minute review — 8 slots this month",book the call

Map the journey on paper (a trigger, a line per message, branch arrows) before configuring anything in the tool. The tool is the last step, not the first.

5.4 Deliverability

Deliverability is the work of getting to the inbox at all: authenticate your domain, warm up the sending identity, and keep complaints and bounces low. None of the clever copy matters if the mailbox providers file it under promotions or spam.

SPF, DKIM and DMARC

The three DNS records prove the email is from you. SPF declares which servers may send for your domain; DKIM signs the message with a public key; DMARC tells receivers what to do when authentication fails. All three are TXT records, and the setup is a copy-paste job in your DNS panel — the failure mode is doing SPF and skipping the other two, which leaves spoofing open. Validate with a tool or `nslookup -type=TXT` after you publish, because a typo in a TXT record fails silently: mail still sends, it just lands in spam.

SPF   TXT  v=spf1 include:_spf.yourmailer.com ~all
DKIM  TXT  mail._domainkey.yourdomain.com  v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=LONG_PUBLIC_KEY
DMARC TXT  _dmarc.yourdomain.com  v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com

Reputation and throttling

Inbox placement follows a reputation score built from bounces, complaints, and engagement. The practical rules are simple numbers: keep hard bounces under 2% per send, complaints under 0.1%, and avoid volume spikes — mailbox providers throttle mail that jumps to many times the 30-day average, so a "big blast" after weeks of quiet gets throttled precisely when it needs to land. Warm up a new sending domain gradually (small volumes rising over weeks), keep a plain-text version in every campaign, and never send from a shared IP without checking the neighbours' reputation. The checklist below is the pre-send gate, and it should fail sends, not just inform them.

Before every send:
[ ] SPF, DKIM and DMARC verified in DNS
[ ] Hard bounce rate below 2% over the last 3 sends
[ ] Spam complaints under 0.1% of sends
[ ] Plain-text version present; images carry alt text
[ ] Volume within 2x of the 30-day average
[ ] Unsubscribe link visible in the first screen of the mail
[ ] Spam-trigger words checked (ALL CAPS, "free money", urgency)

A single bad send can freeze a domain for weeks, and the recovery is slow, not instant: you rebuild reputation with volume and engagement, not with an apology email. Protect the identity like the asset it is.

5.5 Subject Lines, A/B Testing and Compliance

Subject lines decide whether the email is opened; honest mechanics and consent decide whether the campaign is legal. Both are testable and checkable — never a matter of taste.

Testing subject lines

Test subject lines with a split at send time: two to three variants, a sample of at least a few thousand per variant, and a stop rule at statistical significance — 95% is the standard bar, and peeking early is how false winners get shipped. Rotate the test lower down the funnel too: the subject line that wins on a warm list (specific and polite) rarely wins on a cold one (curiosity and loss aversion). Keep a matrix of what was tested, so the pattern of what your audience opens becomes a reusable asset rather than a forgotten experiment.

variant,subject,opens,sent,open rate,verdict
A (control),"Your weekly SEO tip sheet is ready",624,2000,31.2%,benchmark
B (curiosity),"The 15-minute fix Google will thank you for",696,2000,34.8%,winner
C (specific),"12 Google Business settings to check today",668,2000,33.4%,runner-up

Run: split at send time, 2,000 per variant, stop at 95% significance.
No peeking at results before the planned sample is complete.

GDPR and CAN-SPAM essentials

Compliance is not a legal footnote, it is the permission framework the whole channel runs on. GDPR (applying to anyone whose data you hold in the EU/UK, wherever you are) requires explicit, documented consent — the double opt-in record from 5.1 is exactly that record — and an instant, one-click unsubscribe honoured without a login or a survey. CAN-SPAM (US senders and recipients) requires accurate sender identity, honest subject lines, a physical postal address in the mail, and opt-outs honoured within ten business days. The two laws differ in machinery and agree in spirit: no deceptive subject lines, no bought lists, no unmarked adverts, and an unsubscribe that works. The compliant footer below is the template; ship it in every campaign, including the automated sequences.

<p>You are receiving this because you asked for the weekly SEO tips.</p>
<p><a href="{{unsubscribe}}">Unsubscribe instantly</a> —
no login, no survey, one click.</p>
<p>Acme Roofing Ltd · 14 Station Road · Birmingham B1 2XY · UK
· hello@example.com</p>

"Unsubscribe anytime" must be true in the tool, not just in the copy — and the suppression list must be honoured across every list in the account. One complaint after a supposed unsubscribe is a trust breach and a reputation hit.

6 ▸ Paid Acquisition

6.1 Google Ads Search (PPC)6.2 Meta Ads6.3 The Auction and Bidding6.4 Landing Pages and Conversion Tracking6.5 Budget Allocation and ROAS

Paid acquisition buys attention at scale: Google Ads captures people already searching, Meta Ads reaches people who have not searched yet. Both run on auctions, live or die on landing pages and tracking, and get funded by return on ad spend. This chapter covers the two big platforms, the auction mechanics, conversion measurement, and the budget rules.

6.1 Google Ads Search (PPC)

Search ads meet existing intent: someone types a query and your ad can answer it. The account's structure — campaign, ad groups, keywords — determines how much control you have over who sees what.

Account structure

Structure Google Ads as campaign, then ad groups, then keywords. Each ad group carries keywords, ads and landing pages that all share one theme — a "roof repair" ad group with a "roof repair" ad pointing at a roof-repair landing page. The tight theme is the whole game: highly relevant groups earn higher quality scores, which lowers the price you pay per click. Keep match types honest, add negative keywords ("jobs", "free", "DIY") at account level, and start with Search only — Performance Max and Display are learning instruments, not first steps for a small advertiser.

Account: Acme Roofing — Search
└─ Campaign: Search — Roof Repair (target metro, $40/day)
    ├─ Ad group 1: "roof repair" (exact + phrase)
    │    ├─ Ad: "Storm-damaged roof? Fixed within 48 hours"
    │    └─ Keywords: roof repair, roof repair near me
    ├─ Ad group 2: "leak repair" (exact + phrase)
    └─ Ad group 3: "emergency roofing" (phrase)
Shared: negative list "jobs, free, DIY" + sitelink extensions

Match types

Match types decide how literally a query must match your keyword. Exact match (brackets) anchors the account and should carry most of the budget once you know your terms; phrase match (quotes) adds moderate reach; broad match is a research instrument that must be watched every day and guarded with negatives, because it can match "roof repair" to "roofing material for sale". The common mistake is starting with broad and bleeding for two weeks before adding negatives. The symbols are the syntax — get them right in the builder and audit them monthly.

keyword type,written as,example query it can match,use
exact,[roof repair],roof repair,anchor of the account
phrase,"roof repair",roof repair birmingham,moderate reach
broad,roof repair,roofing repair service,capture misspellings, watch daily
negative,-jobs,roof repair jobs,cuts waste from day one

Tight ad groups beat giant ones. If an ad group's keywords cannot be written into one sentence, split it; the ad relevance and quality score will pay you back in cheaper clicks.

6.2 Meta Ads

Meta Ads target people, not queries: the platform decides who is most likely to respond based on behaviour signals, so audiences and creative matter more than keywords. The structure mirrors Google's, but the lever is different.

Ad account and objectives

Meta's hierarchy is campaign, ad set, ad — with an objective chosen per campaign: awareness, traffic or leads. Pick leads and let the delivery system optimise for conversions once the pixel has data; the system needs the conversion event (chapter 6.4) firing correctly before it can optimise toward it. Ad sets carry the targeting and the budget; put several ad sets in one campaign and let the campaign budget shift to the winner — that automatic shift is the mechanism that finds your audience without you guessing.

Ad account → campaigns
Campaign: "Spring — Local Leads (Meta)", objective: leads
├─ Ad set 1: 10km around metro, age 25-54, homeowners
│    ├─ Ad: carousel — 3 services, CTA "Get a quote"
│    └─ Ad: single image before/after, CTA "Book a free check"
├─ Ad set 2: lookalike of past bookers (1-3%), national
└─ Ad set 3: retargeting — visited the booking page, not converted

Budget rule: ad sets inherit the campaign budget and shift to winners weekly.

Audiences and creative

Define an audience with three layers — location and demographics, interests or behaviours, and the signal layer (custom audiences, lookalikes, retargeting). Keep the audience wide enough for the delivery system to learn: a few tens of thousands, not a few thousand, or optimization starves. Creative is the other half of the auction: square or vertical video of 6–15 seconds, readable with sound off, one CTA, two hooks tested in the first three seconds. The ad that wins is the one the audience scrolls past least, and the only honest test is the auction itself.

Audience: "Local Homeowners"
  Location: 10 km radius of the city centre
  Age 25-54, any gender
  Interests: home improvement, garden, DIY
  Behaviour: engaged shoppers; mobile active after 6pm
  Broad targeting: on, to let the system learn

Creative spec: square 1:1 video 6-15s, text overlay readable
on mute, single CTA, logo top corner, two hooks tested in the
first 3 seconds.

6.3 The Auction and Bidding

Every impression is an auction: your bid competes with quality, and the price you pay is what it takes to beat the challenger — not your own bid. Understanding the auction is what stops you overpaying for impressions.

How the auction works

Ad rank is bid multiplied by quality — click-through rate, landing page experience, ad relevance. The winner is not the highest bid but the highest rank, and the price is "the minimum needed to beat the next advertiser", which is usually less than your own bid. That is why quality is a discount, not a bonus: a 7/10 quality score at $3.50 beats a 5/10 at $4.00. Etch the arithmetic in your head so the weekly "why did my CPC rise?" conversation starts with quality, not with the bid slider.

Ad rank = bid × quality  (quality: CTR, landing page, relevance)
You pay: the lowest amount needed to beat the next ad,
per click (CPC) or per 1000 impressions (CPM).

Example:
You bid $3.50 with quality 7/10   → rank 24.5
Rival bids $4.00 with quality 5/10 → rank 20.0
You win, and typically pay just over the rival's price,
not your own $3.50 bid.

Choosing a bid strategy

Bid strategies trade control for automation. Manual CPC teaches you what a click is worth before you let the machine spend; Max clicks buys volume first (set a strict cap); Target CPA is the workhorse once you have conversion history, because the system forecasts and bids per auction toward that cost; Target ROAS needs revenue-per-conversion tracking, so it is only trustworthy after value is wired in (chapter 6.4 and chapter 7). The rule: start manual, graduate to Target CPA, and treat any strategy change as a test with a 2–4 week window before judging it.

strategy,when,what it does,risk
Manual CPC,learning the account,set your own max CPC per keyword,slow, controlled
Maximize clicks,volume first,Google spends to gather clicks,no target guard
Target CPA,profitable CPA known,automated bidding to that number,needs conversion data
Target ROAS,revenue per conversion known,automated bidding toward ROAS,needs reliable value tracking
Portfolio,several campaigns,one goal shared across them,manage carefully

Do not chase impressions or clicks — chase the conversion event. An account full of clicks with no tracked conversions is an account flying blind, and bid automation on top of it is automating a guess.

6.4 Landing Pages and Conversion Tracking

Ads earn clicks; landing pages earn conversions; tracking proves it. The three must agree: the ad's promise, the page's headline, and the event that records success are the same sentence in three places.

Message match and speed

The landing page exists to complete the ad's promise and capture the lead — nothing else. The headline should match the ad almost word for word (a mismatch is a bounce generator), the form should ask for the minimum, and the page should carry no navigation menu, because every other link is a place to leave. Speed is a conversion lever, not a nicety: a page that loads in 5 seconds on mobile loses the majority of the traffic it bought. The checklist below is a gate, not an aspiration — launch nothing that fails it.

[ ] Headline matches the ad's promise, word for word
[ ] One call to action above the fold, repeated below
[ ] Form asks for the minimum (email or phone + one qualifier)
[ ] Loads in under 3 seconds on a 4G mobile connection
[ ] Trust: review quote, guarantee, or certification near the CTA
[ ] No navigation menu — the only way out is the CTA
[ ] Thank-you page confirms the next step and its timing
[ ] Conversion event fires on the thank-you page only

Conversion tracking

Fire the conversion event on the thank-you page, not the form page — a submit that errors is not a lead. Pass event parameters (value, currency, source, campaign) so analytics can later compute ROAS and attribute properly (chapter 7 does the math). Test the event in debug view after publish: the most common silent failure is the event firing on every page because the snippet was placed in the site header instead of the thank-you page. A tracking gap is worse than no tracking, because it manufactures confidence in a channel that is actually losing money.

<script>
  // Place on the thank-you page only — fires once per conversion
  gtag('event', 'generate_lead', {
    value: 50,
    currency: 'USD',
    source: 'googleads',
    campaign: 'roof-repair-search'
  });
</script>

Track a micro-conversion too — clicks from the landing page to the booking tool. If the page converts fine but the booking tool is a wall, the fix is not more ads; it is the tool.

6.5 Budget Allocation and ROAS

Spend follows the numbers: the marginal return per channel decides where the next dollar goes, and ROAS is the ruler. Both need four weeks of data before they are trusted.

Allocating by marginal return

Budget allocation is a monthly decision made from the last four weeks of spend and revenue per channel. The rule of motion: shift 10% from underperformers to the winner, capped so no channel grows more than 20% in one step — a channel that suddenly doubles is a channel you have changed the test conditions for. Watch the cap on the retargeting channel twice: it often shows the best ROAS precisely because it is small, and scaling it 10x dilutes the audience and kills the number.

channel,last-4w spend,last-4w revenue,ROAS,next-month share
Google Search,$1600,$8640,5.4x,+10% to $1760
Meta leads,$800,$3120,3.9x,hold at $800
YouTube,$400,$880,2.2x,-25% to $300
Retargeting,$200,$1720,8.6x,double to $400, watch the scale limit

Rule: shift only after 4 weeks of data, in steps —
never cut a channel that feeds the others.

ROAS math

ROAS is revenue divided by ad spend, and its honest floor is break-even: ROAS 1 at a 40% gross margin, because 1 divided by 0.40 is 2.5 — you need $2.50 of revenue per $1 of spend just to cover what it costs you to deliver. Add the profit uplift the business wants and the target becomes break-even times the uplift. Never discuss ad spend alone: a campaign with a glorious revenue number and a 1.2 ROAS at 40% margin is a loser wearing a win. Decimal places matter less than the story — ROAS is only meaningful next to the margin it funds.

ROAS = revenue / ad spend

Break-even ROAS = 1 / gross margin
Example: margin 40% → break-even = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5
You need $2.50 revenue per $1 spent to break even.

Target = break-even × (1 + required profit uplift)
At 40% margin and 20% uplift: 2.5 × 1.20 = 3.0

ROAS without margin is half the story. The same $1.00 of spend can be profit at an 80% margin and a loss at 30% — always pair the two numbers in the report.

Analytics closes the loop the strategy opened: GA4 measures events, attribution decides which touchpoint gets credit, reporting turns data into decisions on a cadence, experimentation replaces opinion with evidence, and scaling invests more into what the numbers proved. 7.1 Measurement with GA4

Google Analytics 4 is event-based: everything is an event with parameters, and "conversions" are just events you designate as key. Define the event schema before you build reports, or every report inherits the guessing.

Events and key events

GA4 collects page views and enhanced events automatically; the schema you own is the business layer — which events mean progress through your funnel, and which one is the key event you optimise toward. Keep the names stable and documented; renaming an event mid-quarter silently resets your trend. Designate the money event (a form submit, a booked call) as a key event in Admin, because key events are what automated bid strategies and ROAS reports anchor to. Value parameters make the math possible later: send currency and value on the event, and GA4 can approximate revenue per channel.

event_name,params,when it fires
page_view,page_location,entered,every page load (automatic)
scroll,percent_scrolled,at 90% scroll depth (enhanced)
form_start,form_id,first interaction with the form
form_submit,form_id,successful submit
generate_lead,value/currency/method,thank-you page (key event)

Mark generate_lead as a key event in Admin → Key events.

Custom dimensions and user properties

Custom dimensions add your own context to events — plan tier, signup channel, traffic source override — and user properties tag the person behind the events. Set user properties in the config tag so they ride along with every event for a session. The discipline is registering dimensions before you need them: GA4 does not backfill, so a dimension added after a quarter of data is a report that starts three months late. Keep the dimension list small and named in kebab-case; every extra dimension is a reporting option nobody uses.

<script>
  // Configure the tag with user properties sent on every event
  gtag('config', 'G-XXXXXXXXXX', {
    user_properties: {
      plan: 'free',
      signup_channel: 'organic'
    }
  });
</script>

GA4's default conversion model and sampling can mislead on big audiences; for spend decisions, prefer the exported or server-side event stream over a sampled exploration. Sampling shows a picture, not a receipt.

7.2 Attribution Models

Attribution decides which touchpoint gets credit for a conversion — and credit decides budget. The model you pick changes which channel looks like the winner, so pick it deliberately and note the change in reports.

The common models

Last-click (the default in most tools) gives everything to the last touchpoint and flatters the bottom of the funnel; first-click flatters the top; linear splits evenly and is noisy; position-based gives 40% to each end and 20% to the middle, which matches how real journeys feel; time-decay rewards recency and suits long sales cycles; data-driven lets the platform redistribute by learned patterns once you have enough events (thousands). There is no correct model — there is the model that matches your sales cycle and your budget question. The sin is changing models mid-comparison without flagging it.

model,who gets credit,use when
First-click,the first touchpoint,cold/first impression matters
Last-click,the last touchpoint,simple; default; favours BOFU
Linear,everyone splits evenly,balanced but noisy
Position-based,40/20/40 to first and last,awareness AND conversion matter
Data-driven,algorithm redistributes,7,000+ events available in GA4
Time-decay,recent touchpoints win,long sales cycles

Reading a real journey

Print one real journey and walk it: organic search found the blog, the newsletter opened twice, a Facebook ad was clicked, a search ad was clicked days later, and direct converted. Under last-click the search ad gets the sale; under position-based the organic visit and the direct end get 40% each and the two ads split the middle. The insight is not which model is right — it is that the funnel worked as a system, and cutting the "invisible" top-of-funnel touches will quietly starve the bottom. When a budget review says "organic does nothing", this is the table to bring.

session,channel,event,days before conversion
101,google_organic,page_view,21
101,email_newsletter,open,14
101,facebook_ads,click,9
101,google_ads,click,3
101,direct,conversion,0

Last-click: google_ads gets the sale.
Position-based: google_organic and direct split 40% each;
email_newsletter and facebook_ads share the middle 20%.

Changing the attribution model changes which campaigns look profitable. When you switch, re-run the last quarter under both models and write the delta into the report — the shift itself is news.

7.3 Reporting Cadence

Reports exist to make decisions at the right time, so each cadence answers one question and serves one audience. A weekly firehose to the CEO is spam; a monthly silence to the team is negligence.

Weekly, monthly, quarterly

Weekly belongs to the marketing team: one page, the top eight KPIs, and a verdict — on track or off track, with the single biggest lever. Monthly belongs to leadership: budget versus actual, three learnings, one next-month decision. Quarterly is the strategy review: channel scorecard, attribution model check, and the plan for next quarter. Every report should fit one page and answer "so what"; if a reader must ask what to do next, the report failed. Block the calendar slots before the month starts, or reporting is the first thing a busy month deletes.

cadence,audience,one question it answers,format
Weekly,marketing team,are we on track?,1 page, top 8 KPIs, verdict
Monthly,leadership,what worked and what changed,budget vs actual + 3 learnings
Quarterly,exec team,is the strategy still right?,channel scorecard + next-quarter plan
Ad hoc,channel owners,was the test a winner?,one page per test

The one-page dashboard

Keep one dashboard per business, not one per tool: metric, target, actual, trend arrow, owner. Targets come from chapter 2 (the KPI definitions), actuals from the tools, and the trend arrow is the weekly conversation starter. The value is the exceptions — a dashboard where everything is green is either a great quarter or a dashboard nobody updates; ask which. Same KPI, same definition, same source every single week; the moment the definition wobbles, the trend line lies.

7.4 Experimentation and CRO

Experimentation replaces opinion with evidence, and CRO (conversion rate optimisation) is experimentation applied to the conversion path. Every test needs a hypothesis, a plan and a stop rule — a test without a stop rule is an opinion with a timer.

The hypothesis framework

Write the test as: changing X to Y will move KPI Z by N percent, because reason. The "because" is what makes it a hypothesis instead of a tweak, and it forces you to name the mechanism — benefit-stated-before-click, fewer fields, clearer proof. Score candidate tests with ICE (Impact, Confidence, Ease, each 1–10) so the pipeline runs on the best-scoring experiments, not the loudest. Multiply the three scores and run the top of the list; the queue discipline is what stops the team testing whatever crossed someone's mind at lunch.

ICE = Impact × Confidence × Ease  (each 1-10, out of 1000)

Hypothesis: changing the CTA button from "Submit" to
"Get my free quote" will raise form completion by 10%,
because the benefit is stated before the click.

Impact: 6   Confidence: 5   Ease: 8   ICE = 240
Test: A/B on the booking page, 50/50, 4 weeks or 4,000 visitors.

Running a valid test

A valid test changes one variable, runs to a planned sample, and is read at significance — 95% is the standard bar, 90% for cheap exploratory bets. The three killer sins: peeking at results daily and stopping on the first green (false winners ship this way), testing three changes at once so no change is responsible for the win, and declaring a winner from a 1% lift on 200 visitors. Plan the traffic needed before launch: 4,000 visitors divided 50/50 finds a 5–8% lift; it will not find a 2% one, and pretending it will is how the dashboard lies.

element,control,variant,traffic,min lift,decision rule
CTA text,Submit,Get my free quote,50/50 split,5%,adopt if p ≥ 95%
Form fields,6 fields,3 fields,50/50 split,8%,adopt if p ≥ 95%,
                                              no lead-quality drop
Headline,as-is,benefit-led,50/50 split,4%,adopt if p ≥ 90%

Rules: one variable at a time; no peeking; run to the planned
sample before reading results.

Stack the wins: every adopted test graduates into the control of the next test, so the conversion path improves monotonically and the experiment log becomes a changelog of the funnel.

7.5 Scaling and Playbooks

Scaling is not spending more — it is investing more into what the numbers proved, and writing down how, so the growth survives staff changes and channel changes. The playbook is the scaling unit.

What to scale

Scale on the evidence: a channel with ninety days of above-target ROAS gets budget; a channel at break-even gets held and re-tested, not doubled. The table below is the decision format — channel, spend, ROAS over 90 days, and an explicit scale decision with a guardrail (watch frequency, watch CPA). Guardrails are the discipline: scaling retargeting without watching ad frequency is how a friendly channel turns creepy at 5 exposures a day, and scaling search without watching the CPA cliff is how a winner becomes a loss in two increments of 20%.

channel,current spend,ROAS 90d,scale?,action
Google Search,$1.6k/month,5.4x,yes,+20%, watch the CPA cliff
Meta leads,$800/month,3.9x,maybe,+10%, refresh creative weekly
YouTube,$400/month,2.2x,no,hold; fix the offer math first
Retargeting,$200/month,8.6x,yes capped,'+50% max, watch frequency ≥ 3/day

Growth loops

A growth loop is a funnel that feeds itself: the output of one cycle is the input of the next. The classic services loop: content answers a question, the reader joins the list, the welcome emails prove the method, the call books, the client's story becomes the next case study, and the case study feeds the blog. Loops beat funnels at scale because each cycle adds fuel instead of consuming it — which is why the loop's KPI (case studies per quarter, leads from them) is the number to protect, not the monthly traffic. If the loop is a chicken-and-egg, start the cycle wherever you already get attention.

Loop for a services brand:
  Content answers a question
    → reader joins the email list
      → welcome emails prove the method
        → booked call
          → the client story becomes the next case study
            → case study feeds the blog  (back to the top)

Loop KPIs: case studies per quarter (target 5),
leads attributed to case studies (target 12 per quarter).

The playbook document

A playbook is the repeatable procedure for one channel or tactic: owner, goal, cadence, numbered steps that are copy-paste safe, and two gates — launch gates (what must be true to start) and failure gates (what makes it stop, and who decides). Teams that document playbooks survive their first departure and their first platform algorithm change; teams that keep procedures in one person's head restart from zero every time. Keep the playbook in one file, review it quarterly, and treat the failure gate as sacred: a playbook without a stop rule is a subscription to a strategy that stopped working last year.

# Playbook: [channel or tactic]
Owner:        [name]
Goal:         [the KPI this moves]
Cadence:      [weekly / monthly]
Steps:        1..N, numbered, copy-paste safe
Checklist:    [ ] launch gates    [ ] failure gates

Launch gate: what must be true to start
Failure gate: what makes it stop, and who decides
Last reviewed: [date]
